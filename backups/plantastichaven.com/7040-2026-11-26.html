A useful plant-care guide is a repeatable record, not a fixed watering calendar. For each plant, note its species, light level, pot and drainage, soil mix, last thorough watering, feeding, repotting, symptoms, and the next observation date. Adjust care to the plant and room rather than watering every plant on the same schedule.

How to use this checklist

Start with the plant record, add observations as conditions change, and choose the next check from what you see. The worksheet is useful for a single plant or a collection; it is not a promise to water on a fixed interval.

Plant record

  • Plant identity: common name, scientific name if known, and a photo/date.
  • Location: room, window direction, distance from the window, and light notes.
  • Container: pot size, material, drainage hole, saucer, and cachepot details.
  • Growing medium: soil or mix description and any amendments.
  • Care history: last thorough watering, fertilizer, repotting, pruning, and pest treatment.
  • Next check: choose a date based on the plant and conditions, not a universal watering rule.

Routine checklist

Weekly observation

  • [ ] Check the mix and pot for moisture in a way that suits the plant.
  • [ ] Look at leaves, stems, and undersides for changes or pests.
  • [ ] Check that the drainage hole is open and the saucer or cachepot is not holding water.
  • [ ] Note changes in light, temperature, drafts, heating, or air-conditioning.
  • [ ] Record what you observed, what you did, and when you will look again.

Monthly review

  • [ ] Wipe dust from leaves when appropriate for the plant.
  • [ ] Review fertilizer use and the growing season.
  • [ ] Check whether roots, pot size, or mix behavior justify repotting.
  • [ ] Update the plant photo and compare observations over time.

Plant care checklist FAQ

How often should I use the checklist?

Use the observation sections as often as needed for the plant and season. The checklist is a record, not a rigid schedule.

Can I use it for succulents?

Yes. Record the pot, drainage, light, mix, and drying observations, then adapt the next check to the plant. See the succulent soil mix guide for related decisions.

What if I do not know the plant name?

Record the best description you have, add a photo, and note the room and care history. Identification can be improved later without losing useful observations.

Is there a PDF?

🧰 Three Tools That Make This Checklist Easier

You can run this whole routine with nothing but your hands and a watering can. These three just remove the guesswork from the parts people most often get wrong.

  • Soil moisture meter — the highest-impact purchase for anyone who has lost a plant to overwatering. Sticking a finger in the top inch tells you very little in a deep or dense pot, where the root zone can still be saturated. Choose a single-function analogue probe long enough to reach the roots.
  • Digital hygrometer — settles the humidity question before you spend money on a humidifier. Most tropical houseplants are comfortable between 40 and 60% relative humidity, and many homes blamed for dry air are already in range.
  • Long-spout watering can — lets you water the soil rather than the crown and foliage, which is how a lot of fungal leaf spotting starts.

What to skip: 3-in-1 meters that claim to read pH and light as well — the pH readings are unreliable in potting mix. And self-watering globes, which empty unpredictably and manage to cause both drought and root rot.
