Why is my philodendron turning brown?

Brown philodendron leaves develop when leaf tissue is damaged by water stress, excessive light, mineral salts, cold, pests or disease. Crispy tips and edges usually suggest a moisture-balance problem or salt injury; soft, wet-looking brown spots that expand warrant immediate isolation and a disease check. Brown leaves accompanied by wilting in wet soil call for a root inspection, not more watering.

First move: feel the potting mix below the surface, check whether the pot drains, then inspect both sides of the damaged leaf. The pattern and the growing conditions together are more useful than color alone.

A five-minute inspection before you water or treat

  1. Check the damage. Is it dry and crisp, soft and wet, or scarred and speckled? Note whether only old leaves, new growth or the window-facing side is affected.
  2. Check the root zone. Feel the mix below the surface and lift the pot. Is the pot unusually heavy or very light? Check for blocked drainage holes and water trapped inside a decorative outer pot.
  3. Check the room. Look for a new sunny location, a radiator, an air-conditioner draft, a cold window or a recent change in watering frequency.
  4. Inspect for pests. Use a magnifier around the leaf undersides, veins, petioles and folded new leaves. Tap a leaf above white paper to look for moving mites.
  5. Record a baseline. Take a photo and note which leaves are damaged. Isolate a plant with rapidly spreading wet spots or visible pests before moving on.

Do not assume that a wilted plant needs water. Roots in a saturated pot may be unable to supply the leaves even when water is abundant. The University of Maryland Extension root-rot guide explains this important distinction.

What causes brown philodendron leaves, and how to fix each cause

1. Excess water and root damage

Philodendrons need both water and air around their roots. Dense mix, an oversized pot or water held in an outer container can keep the root zone saturated. Inspect the roots if the plant wilts despite wet soil, has a soft stem base or declines progressively. Healthy roots are generally firm; dark roots that collapse or feel mushy are more concerning than color alone.

What to do: stop adding water while the mix is saturated. Empty the saucer or outer pot. If roots are decaying, remove clearly rotted material with clean tools and repot the surviving healthy root system in fresh, well-draining houseplant mix. Use a container sized for the remaining roots, not for the leaf canopy. Severely diseased plants may not be salvageable. Read the philodendron soil mix guide for substrate and drainage choices.

2. Underwatering, uneven wetting and cramped roots

If the mix is dry and the pot feels unusually light, crispy leaf edges may reflect a lack of water. A root-bound plant or a dried-out mix can also allow water to run around the root ball while its center stays dry. Check the center rather than relying on a wet surface.

What to do: rewet dry mix gradually, water until excess drains and empty the outer pot. Recheck moisture instead of watering on a fixed schedule. If tightly packed roots leave little room for mix, assess whether repotting is needed. See our philodendron watering guide and the University of Maryland Extension guide to pot-bound plants.

3. Dry air, drafts and interrupted leaf expansion

Dry, warm air near heaters or direct airflow from an air conditioner can increase moisture loss. New leaves may develop dry edges or damaged areas while unfolding. However, crispy edges alone do not prove low humidity; first check root moisture and salt accumulation.

What to do: move the plant away from strong drafts and use a hygrometer if dry air is suspected. If necessary, increase room humidity gradually while maintaining airflow. Do not keep leaves continuously wet or raise humidity around a plant with suspicious spreading lesions.

4. Excessive sunlight or cold injury

Direct, intense sunlight can cause localized faded patches that become dry and brown. A philodendron recently moved from a dim room to a sunny window is particularly worth checking. Cold glass, cold drafts or transport in winter can also injure tissue. Poor light, in contrast, may indirectly worsen root problems by reducing water use.

What to do: provide stable, filtered light and protect foliage from temperature extremes. Move the plant gradually rather than repeatedly changing location. Use the philodendron light guide and cold-tolerance guide to choose a suitable position.

5. Fertilizer burn and mineral accumulation

Too much fertilizer or mineral accumulation can damage the roots and leaf margins. Look for pale crust on the growing medium or pot, a history of frequent feeding, and recurring tip burn. A brown margin is not a reason to add more fertilizer.

What to do: suspend feeding while the plant is stressed. If the root system is healthy and the container drains well, flush accumulated salts with low-mineral water and allow complete drainage. If the mix is already waterlogged or roots are compromised, address that problem first. Follow label rates when feeding resumes. See University of Maryland Extension on fertilizer toxicity.

6. Thrips, spider mites and other pests

Thrips may leave silvery or bronze scars, black waste specks and distorted growth; spider mites often produce fine stippling and sometimes delicate webbing. Check the leaf undersides and young leaves, where an early infestation can be easy to overlook.

What to do: isolate the plant, examine nearby plants and identify the pest before choosing a treatment. Mild infestations may respond to physical removal or washing when appropriate. For persistent pests, choose a product labeled for the plant, pest and indoor use, and follow its directions. Avoid improvised detergent sprays and unnecessary mixtures. Read University of Minnesota Extension's indoor pest guide and our philodendron pests and diseases guide.

7. Spreading brown spots and possible infection

Brown spots that begin as water-soaked areas, enlarge, develop a yellow margin or are associated with collapsing tissue require prompt attention. Philodendrons can develop bacterial leaf spots and soft rots as well as fungal diseases. Dry tan spots can have noninfectious causes, so a photograph alone cannot establish the organism responsible.

What to do: isolate the plant, avoid splashing water between plants, keep leaves dry and sanitize tools after handling affected tissue. Remove heavily affected leaves if the damage is localized and removal is practical. If lesions spread rapidly, petioles collapse or the stem develops soft, foul-smelling tissue, seek advice from a local plant clinic; severely infected plants may need to be discarded to protect the collection. Do not assume a fungicide will cure bacterial disease.

The Connecticut Agricultural Experiment Station's Philodendron plant-health handbook and University of California IPM houseplant guidance describe these disease patterns and precautions.

8. Natural aging or physical damage

An older lower leaf can yellow and brown as a healthy plant replaces it. A leaf may also retain a brown scar after transport, rubbing against a surface or damage during unfolding. Look for stable damage and continued healthy new growth. Repeated browning on successive leaves is a stronger reason to investigate growing conditions.

Which philodendron are you growing?

Care adjustments should reflect the plant's growth habit and foliage. A heartleaf philodendron and a large, self-heading cultivar do not necessarily dry at the same rate in the same room.

  • Heartleaf and Brasil: inspect the root ball and light exposure before assuming every brown tip is dehydration. NC State Extension notes that heartleaf philodendron tolerates relatively low light but can suffer root rot in overly wet soil. See our heartleaf care guide.
  • Pink Princess, White Knight and Birkin: pay close attention to recent changes in light. Pale variegated tissue may show damage readily, but spreading wet lesions should never be dismissed as variegation. Compare the Pink Princess guide or Birkin guide.
  • Micans, Gloriosum and other velvety-leaved types: inspect expanding leaves, moisture balance and airflow; look underneath foliage for pests before changing humidity or watering. Read the Micans care guide and Gloriosum care guide.

A 14-day recovery tracker

Brown, dead leaf tissue will not turn green again. The recovery goal is to stop new injury and protect healthy growth, not to make old brown patches disappear.

Day 0: establish the cause

Photograph both sides of affected leaves. Check pot moisture, drainage, light and pests. Address active rot or infection immediately.

Days 1–3: stabilize

Apply the cause-specific correction. For a stable noninfectious problem, avoid changing several environmental factors at once.

Days 4–7: reassess

Compare the original photos. Watch for new spots, spreading borders, wilting in wet soil or fresh pest activity. Escalate promptly if these occur.

Days 8–14: evaluate

If damage has stopped spreading, keep conditions stable. Healthy new growth is a longer-term sign; two weeks is a review point, not a recovery guarantee.

Example: a philodendron with crispy margins and dry mix needs a different intervention from one with identical-looking margins but a persistently wet pot. If both plants are watered again indiscriminately, the second may deteriorate. The soil check determines the next action.

Frequently asked questions

Should I cut off brown philodendron leaves?

Keep leaves that are largely green unless an active infection requires removal. Trim dry dead tips for appearance with clean scissors, or remove fully dead leaves. Avoid removing most of the canopy from a stressed plant at once.

Can brown philodendron leaves turn green again?

No. Dead brown tissue does not regenerate. Correct the underlying problem and judge improvement by the absence of new damage and the quality of future growth.

Why are my philodendron leaves turning brown even though the soil is wet?

Excess moisture may have reduced oxygen around the roots or caused root decay. Damaged roots cannot supply the leaves properly. Inspect drainage and root condition before adding more water.

Are brown spots on philodendron leaves contagious?

Not all brown spots are contagious. Sun scorch, physical damage and salt injury do not spread between plants, whereas some pathogens and pests do. Isolate a plant when spots are wet-looking, enlarging or accompanied by signs of pests until the cause is clearer.

Why are the edges of new philodendron leaves brown?

Inspect moisture during leaf expansion, exposure to drafts, root health and pests in folded new leaves. Do not force a stuck leaf open; allow it to unfold naturally and investigate repeated damage.

Should I fertilize a philodendron with brown leaves?

Not until you have identified the cause. Fertilizer does not repair dead tissue, and extra salts can worsen root stress. Resume normal feeding when the root system and active growth support it.
