How to Get Rid of Mice in Walls Safely

Investigate suspected wall activity without cutting exploratory holes or placing loose poison in voids. Confirm accessible physical evidence, use suitable protected traps where rodent routes are supported, and get qualified help when identification or access is uncertain. No checklist can promise that wall opening will never be needed.

Direct Answer: Start with Accessible Evidence, Not Exploratory Holes

Do not cut exploratory holes based on sound alone. To eliminate mice, first inspect safely accessible areas beside the suspected wall, including cabinets, pipe gaps and appliance edges. If mouse activity is confirmed, use mouse-sized snap traps along evidence-supported routes, keep them away from children and pets and check daily.[2][4] Wet-clean accessible waste safely.[3] Do not put loose poison in a void: rodenticides can leave dead rodents inside a structure, creating odor or sanitation problems.[1]

Hearing faint scratching behind your bedroom or kitchen drywall late at night is unsettling, but cutting random exploratory holes creates costly cosmetic damage without solving the root problem. Use the decision aid below to choose what to inspect next; it cannot pinpoint a cavity or route. To learn what the sounds themselves can and cannot tell you, see mouse noises in walls at night.

Mouse Noises in Walls at Night: What You Are Hearing

Scratching or scurrying in a wall at night can be a clue to mouse activity, but sound alone does not prove a mouse, tell you how many there are, or mean you should open the wall. Mice are often active when the house is quiet, so small movements are easier to hear at night. The same sounds can also come from plumbing, ductwork, building expansion or other animals.

  • More likely mice: small, repeating night-time noises in the same spot, together with mouse-sized droppings, food damage, fine nesting material or a gap nearby.
  • Possibly something else: loud thumping, daytime movement or roofline activity may point to a larger animal or a building issue. Check the signs of mice before you act.
  • Record the pattern: room, wall section, time, duration and whether it repeats. A repeating sound paired with nearby evidence tells you more than one unexplained scratch.
  • Stay safe: do not put your face to vents or holes to listen, and do not push cameras or objects into electrical or mechanical openings.

Safe Wall-Mouse Action Plan (Step by Step)

  1. Map all adjacent rooms: Inspect lower cabinets, pantry shelving, and appliance voids directly opposite the noisy wall section.
  2. Cut off food rewards: Store dry food in thick plastic, metal or glass containers with tight lids.[4] Reducing food access supports control; it does not guarantee trapping success.[4][1]
  3. Deploy mechanical snap traps along baseboards: For confirmed mouse activity, follow the trap instructions and place traps perpendicular to evidence-supported wall routes, baited end toward the wall. Keep traps away from children and pets and check daily.[2] Review traps in our best mouse traps guide.
  4. Wet-clean visible droppings safely: Follow CDC ventilation and rubber/plastic glove precautions. Wet waste thoroughly with an appropriate disinfectant and let it soak for 5 minutes or the label time before wiping; do not dry-sweep or vacuum untreated waste.[3] See safe droppings cleanup.
  5. Seal exterior wall penetrations: Inspect and repair suitable entry gaps as part of control while continuing to trap rodents that may remain indoors; do not use five catch-free days as a clearance test. Choose gnaw-resistant repairs appropriate to the opening, and obtain qualified help around utilities. Do not block ventilation, drainage or weep openings.[4][1] See steel wool vs copper mesh.

Void-Bait and Dead-Rodent Risks: What to Know

Warning Against Rodenticide Baits in Walls:

Do not throw loose poison into wall voids. UC IPM notes that rodents poisoned inside buildings may die within the structure and create odor or sanitation problems; accessible trapping allows carcass removal. Any pesticide use must follow its label and protect children, pets and wildlife. Persistent odor or inaccessible contamination needs qualified assessment; neither odor nor a timetable establishes a safe waiting period.[1][4]

Critical Mistakes: What NOT to Do

  • ❌ Do NOT cut exploratory holes into drywall based purely on sound.
  • ❌ Do NOT throw poison blocks into walls, because poisoned rodents may die in inaccessible areas.[1]
  • ❌ Do NOT rely on expanding foam alone for rodent exclusion; select suitable gnaw-resistant repairs.[1][4]
  • ❌ Do NOT dry-sweep or vacuum rodent feces along baseboards.[3]

Can Mice Climb Walls? (Why Vertical Access Matters)

Yes. UC IPM describes house mice as excellent climbers able to run up rough vertical surfaces. This means an inspection should not be limited to foundation-level openings, but climbing ability does not establish the entry point in your home.[1]

Practical takeaway: inspect safely visible exterior gaps at low and high levels, including eaves and soffits. Use qualified help for high or unsafe access. Do not indiscriminately seal ventilation or drainage openings.[4]

Frequently Asked Questions

Can mice live in walls permanently?

Mice may use sheltered spaces for nesting, but sounds alone do not establish a wall nest. House mice can obtain moisture from food and do not necessarily leave daily to drink. Inspect accessible areas for evidence and use suitable traps on confirmed routes rather than assuming a particular exit.[1][2]

How do I get rid of a dead mouse in the wall?

Smell alone cannot establish a carcass or pinpoint a cavity, and there is no universal safe waiting period. Do not cut into a wall based on odor alone. Ask a qualified professional to assess persistent odor, inaccessible contamination and safe access around wiring, plumbing or structural components.[1][4]

How do I trap mice inside walls?

Start with accessible locations where mouse evidence supports placement, such as baseboards, cabinets and spaces behind appliances. Use mouse-sized snap traps according to their instructions, baited end toward the wall, away from children and pets; check daily. A sound does not prove a wall exit. Seek qualified help for inaccessible activity.[2][4]

Do mouse noises in the wall at night mean a large infestation?

No. Sound alone does not tell you how many mice there are. Combine it with accessible evidence (droppings, gnawing, food damage) and a week or two of monitoring before you judge the scale.
