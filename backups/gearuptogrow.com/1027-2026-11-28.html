Short answer: A usable goal names a measurable outcome, the weekly behaviour that produces it, an indicator you can watch in real time, and the obstacle most likely to derail it. Specific, difficult goals outperform vague ones and easy ones on most tasks — but only when the person has the ability and accepts the goal, which is why the obstacle field matters as much as the target.

Best for: people who are working hard and cannot tell whether they are moving, or who set goals that quietly expire without anyone noticing.

Use this when: the behaviour is already possible and you need to decide which behaviour deserves the hours.

Key takeaways

  • Set the outcome, then immediately set the weekly behaviour that produces it. A goal without a behaviour is a hope with a deadline.
  • Watch a lead measure weekly, not the outcome monthly. Outcomes tell you what happened; lead measures tell you what is happening.
  • Every goal needs a stated obstacle and a written response to that obstacle.
  • Difficulty helps up to the point where commitment breaks, then it stops helping.
  • A goal you never review is a goal you have quietly abandoned. Put the review on a calendar before you start.

Three kinds of measure — you need all three

Most goal failures are measurement failures. People track the one number that cannot be influenced directly, then conclude the plan is not working.

Type Example: run a half marathon How often to look
Outcome — the result Finish in under 2:10 At the end. Looking more often is noise
Process — the repeated behaviour Three runs a week, one of them long Weekly. This is the number you control
Lead — an early signal that predicts the outcome Long-run distance rising; easy pace on the same route Weekly or fortnightly

The process measure is the one you act on. The lead measure is the one that tells you whether the process is actually working, which is a different question. A plan of three runs a week that never extends the long run is compliance without progress, and only a lead measure reveals that.

The seven fields every goal needs

Write these out before you start. If a field cannot be filled in, that is the work — not a formality.

Field What it forces you to decide Weak version
1. OutcomeThe single result that defines success"Get healthier"
2. Why it mattersThe reason that survives a bad week"Because I should"
3. MeasureHow the outcome is counted, and by whomA feeling of progress
4. ProcessThe behaviour repeated each week to produce it"Try harder"
5. Lead measureThe early signal that the process is workingNone — the most commonly skipped field
6. ObstacleThe most likely specific cause of failure"Lack of time", which is a category, not a cause
7. Deadline and first reviewWhen it ends and when you first check itA deadline only

Goal-setting theory is unusually well supported here: across hundreds of studies, specific and difficult goals consistently beat vague goals and easy goals. The boundary conditions matter though. The effect depends on the person having the ability to reach the goal and being committed to it, and it weakens or reverses for complex tasks where people are still learning the strategy — on those, a learning goal ("practise three approaches to X") outperforms a performance goal ("hit number Y").

Leaving room for WOOP

The natural pairing with obstacle planning is mental contrasting: spend a few minutes imagining the desired outcome, then immediately imagine the main internal obstacle, then form an if-then plan for it. The evidence here is more mixed than the implementation-intention work — mental contrasting appears to help most when the person's expectation of success is already reasonably high, and can reduce effort when it is low, because it lets you disengage from an unrealistic goal. That is arguably useful information rather than a defect. If vividly imagining the obstacle kills your motivation entirely, the goal may not be one you expect to reach.

Obstacle type Example If-then plan
Internal, situationalLow energy after a late meeting"If the 18:00 meeting overruns, then I run 20 minutes at 07:00 the next day"
Internal, emotionalAvoidance when the task feels unclear"If I open the file and stall, then I write the outline for ten minutes only"
External, competingA colleague asking for something urgent"If someone asks during the block, then it goes on the list and gets answered at 15:00"
External, environmentalThe gym is unusable during peak hours"If it is after 18:00, then the session is the home version"

The review cadence

Reviews are where goals are kept alive or quietly dropped. Four horizons, each answering a different question:

Horizon Time needed The one question it answers
Daily2 minutesDid today's behaviour happen?
Weekly25 minutesDid the process run, and did the lead measure move?
Monthly45 minutesIs the process still the right route to the outcome?
At the deadline60 minutesWhat did the evidence say, and what is the next goal?

The weekly review carries the load; the monthly one is where you are allowed to change the process. If you only keep one, keep the weekly. A written weekly check is also the thing that stops an abandoned goal from sitting on a list for six months looking like a plan.

Worked example: the same goal, built properly

Before: "Get fitter this year." No measure, no process, no review date. Success is decided retrospectively by mood.

Field 1 — outcome: run 10km without walking by 30 June.

Field 2 — why: to have a form of exercise that does not depend on a gym or a booking.

Field 3 — measure: the distance recorded in a running app, checked weekly.

Field 4 — process: three runs a week — two easy 25-minute runs and one longer run.

Field 5 — lead measure: the long-run distance, and whether the same easy route feels easier at the same pace.

Field 6 — obstacle and response: the realistic obstacle is not "time" but Tuesday and Thursday evening fatigue. If-then: "If it is past 19:00 when I get home, then the run is 15 minutes, not 25."

Field 7 — deadline and first review: 30 June, first review this Sunday, 25 minutes, on the calendar.

The second version is not more ambitious. It is just checkable, which means it can be corrected in week three instead of abandoned in week nine.

Where goals go wrong

Failure Why it happens Correction
Too many active goalsEach one is reasonable; together they exceed the available hoursTwo active goals maximum. Others get a written date
Performance goal on a learning taskTargeting a number before you know the strategy increases anxiety, not skillSet a learning goal first, then a performance goal
No lead measureNothing distinguishes compliance from progress until too lateAdd one early indicator you can watch weekly
Goal assigned, not chosenGoal commitment is a pre-condition for the effect, not an optional extraEither find the reason or drop the goal honestly
No review dateWithout a check, drift is invisible and the deadline arrives as a surpriseCalendar the weekly review at the same time you set the goal
Outcome used as the process"Lose 5kg" is a result, not an action, so there is nothing to do todayState the behaviour that produces it

A note on framing: outcome goals reference a comparison, and comparison goals are the ones most likely to sour even when they are hit. If your goal is defined entirely by beating someone else, success becomes contingent on their performance, which you do not control. Where that is unavoidable, pair it with a process measure you do control so the work still has a payoff on a bad day.

Frequently asked questions

How many goals should I have at once?

Two active goals is a reasonable ceiling for most people with a job and a life, and the ceiling is set by hours rather than ambition. Everything else belongs on a dated list. The most common reason a well-built goal fails is that it was never actually competing alone.

Is SMART still worth using?

As a completeness check, yes — it reliably catches goals with no measure and no deadline. As a formulation method it is weaker than it looks, because it says nothing about the behavioural process, the obstacle, or the review. Use it to audit a goal you have already built, not to build one.

What is a lead measure, in plain terms?

An early indicator that the plan is working before the outcome is knowable. For a writing goal, words per session rather than publication date. For fitness, whether a standard route feels easier rather than a race time. If a measure only changes at the end, it cannot help you correct anything.

What if my goal depends on other people?

Split it. Keep a process goal you fully control — the outreach sent, the portfolio extended, the applications submitted — and treat the outcome as a probabilistic consequence rather than a target you can guarantee. This keeps the review meaningful even in months when the outcome is outside your influence.

Should I tell people about my goal?

There is some evidence that announcing an identity-relevant goal can reduce follow-through by producing a premature sense of progress, though the effect is not consistent across studies. The practical version: tell people who will hold you to the process, and be cautious about telling an audience that will applaud the announcement.

How is this different from the self-improvement guide?

Self-improvement decides which area of your life to work on and in what order. This page assumes you have chosen an area and need to specify a result and track it. If you have not yet chosen, the area table there is the right starting point.
