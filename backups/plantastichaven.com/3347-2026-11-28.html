The Honest Answer: No True Purple Spider Plant Exists

So, is there a purple spider plant? The short answer: no — the purple spider plant is not real in the botanical sense; "real or fake" comes down to the label being fake while the plant itself is real: any plant sold under that name is a lookalike from a different genus. There is no naturally occurring spider plant with purple leaves. Chlorophytum comosum (spider plant) comes in green, white-variegated, and curly (bonnie) varieties — all with green as the dominant color. The “purple spider plant” label is a horticultural misnomer that has spread through garden centers and online plant shops.

The plants most commonly mislabeled as purple spider plants are:

  • Purple Heart (Tradescantia pallida): The most common culprit. Deep purple fleshy leaves, trailing growth, small pink flowers. Native to the Gulf Coast of eastern Mexico.
  • Purple Queen (Tradescantia pallida ‘Purpurea’): A named cultivar of Purple Heart with especially intense purple coloring. Very popular as a groundcover in warm climates.
  • Tradescantia zebrina: Purple and silver striped leaves, slightly more delicate than Purple Heart. Very fast-growing and trails aggressively.

If you are seeing purple leaves on a plant labeled “spider plant,” you almost certainly have a Tradescantia — not a spider plant.

True Spider Plant vs Purple Heart: Side by Side

True Spider Plant (Chlorophytum comosum)

The true spider plant is one of the most forgiving and useful houseplants available. Its defining characteristics:

  • Long, arching leaves that fan out from a central rosette
  • Produces long arching stems with baby plantlets at the ends — the most distinctive feature
  • Extremely pet-safe (non-toxic to cats and dogs per ASPCA)
  • Included in NASA's 1989 sealed-chamber air study; later research does not show that houseplants clean the air of an ordinary room
  • Forgiving: tolerates a range of light and some missed waterings, though it still does best with bright indirect light
  • Variegated forms have white-edged leaves; solid green forms are more shade-tolerant

Purple Heart (Tradescantia pallida)

Purple Heart is a beautiful plant in its own right — it just is not a spider plant:

  • Fleshy, deep purple leaves on trailing stems
  • Does not produce plantlets on arching stems
  • Produces small three-petaled pink flowers
  • Mildly toxic to pets — causes gastrointestinal upset if chewed
  • Needs more light than spider plants to maintain purple color
  • Grows much faster than spider plants
  • Propagates from stem cuttings in water or soil

Why Is Your Purple Plant Turning Green?

This is one of the most common questions about “purple spider plants.” The answer is straightforward: your plant needs more light. The purple color in Tradescantia pallida (Purple Heart, Purple Queen) is an anthocyanin pigment that the plant produces in response to bright light. In low light, the plant deprioritizes anthocyanin production and puts energy into chlorophyll instead — which makes the leaves green.

How to restore purple color:

  • Move the plant to a bright spot with at least 4–6 hours of indirect light daily
  • Some direct morning sun is fine; avoid harsh afternoon direct sun
  • In low-light conditions, purple plants will always revert toward green over time — this is normal and not a sign of disease
  • New growth in bright light will emerge purple

For true spider plants, green leaves turning greener (or losing variegation) in low light is also normal — the plant is prioritizing chlorophyll production over pigmented leaf edges.

Propagation Differences

Spider Plant Propagation

Spider plants are the easiest propagation project in houseplant care. Wait until the baby plantlets develop small nubs or aerial roots at their base, then:

  1. Cut the baby plantlet from the mother plant’s stem with clean scissors
  2. Place in a glass of water, roots facing down, for 1–2 weeks until roots are 1–2 inches long
  3. Pot in well-draining aroid mix or standard potting mix with perlite
  4. Keep soil lightly moist for the first 2 weeks

You can also pin the baby to the soil of a nearby pot while still attached to the mother plant — once rooted, cut the stem. This “layering” method has the highest success rate.

Purple Heart / Tradescantia Propagation

Tradescantia propagation is similarly easy but uses stem cuttings:

  1. Cut a 4–6 inch stem section with at least 2–3 leaf nodes
  2. Remove leaves from the bottom 1–2 nodes (these are where roots will emerge)
  3. Place in water or directly into moist soil
  4. Roots appear within 1–2 weeks in water, or within 2–3 weeks in soil

Tradescantia cuttings are so vigorous they will root in plain water without any rooting hormone.

Pet Safety and Handling

Spider plant: Pet-safe. True spider plants (Chlorophytum comosum) are non-toxic to cats and dogs per the ASPCA. Cats are often drawn to the arching leaves and may chew them. The ASPCA lists the plant as non-toxic, but eating a lot of any plant can upset a pet's stomach, so it is still sensible to keep it out of reach of a determined chewer.

Purple Heart and Tradescantia zebrina: Mildly toxic to pets. Tradescantia species are listed as mildly toxic by the ASPCA due to their sap content, which can cause gastrointestinal upset if chewed. For pet-safe purple plants, look for non-toxic options like Hypoestes (polka dot plant), which has pink-, red- or white-spotted foliage and is listed by the ASPCA as non-toxic to cats and dogs.

Purple Spider Wort, Lycoris and Other “Purple Spider” Plants

Searches for a purple spider plant often pull in three other plants that share the nickname. Knowing which one you actually saw saves you from buying the wrong thing:

  • Purple spiderwort (Tradescantia pallida, also sold as purple queen or purple heart) — the trailing purple plant most photos of a “purple spider plant” actually show. Same plant as Purple Heart, just sold under the spiderwort name.
  • Red spider lily (Lycoris radiata) — a bulb plant whose spidery flowers are usually red, sometimes searched as a spider lily. It is unrelated to Chlorophytum and grows from a bulb outdoors, in roughly zones 6 to 10.
  • Spiderwort varieties (Tradescantia spp.) like Ohio spiderwort — clumping purple-flowered perennials for outdoor beds, not houseplant spider plants.

If your “purple spider plant” has fuzzy purple trailing leaves, it is Tradescantia pallida. If it has strappy leaves and bare flower stalks in autumn rather than trailing stems, it is more likely a Lycoris bulb than a houseplant.

FAQ

Is the purple spider plant real or fake?

The purple spider plant is real as a plant, but fake as a spider plant: it is a marketing label. No Chlorophytum comosum cultivar has purple leaves. Plants sold as "purple spider plants" are almost always Purple Heart (Tradescantia pallida) or another trailing purple plant from a different genus.

Is purple spider plant a real spider plant cultivar?

No. There is no naturally occurring spider plant (Chlorophytum comosum) with purple leaves. Plants labeled “purple spider plant” in garden centers and online shops are almost always Purple Heart (Tradescantia pallida) or Tradescantia zebrina — completely different genera. The confusion arises because Tradescantia species have a trailing growth habit similar to spider plants and some have purple-tinged foliage.

Is Purple Heart the same as spider plant?

No. Purple Heart (Tradescantia pallida) and spider plant (Chlorophytum comosum) are in completely different botanical genera, different families, and have different care needs. Spider plants grow in a clumping rosette and produce babies on arching stems; Purple Heart grows as a trailing vine with no plantlets. Both are popular houseplants, but they are not interchangeable or related.

Why does my purple spider plant not make babies?

Because you likely have Purple Heart or another Tradescantia, not a true spider plant. Only Chlorophytum comosum (spider plant) produces characteristic baby plantlets on arching stems. Tradescantia species do not produce plantlets — they propagate from stem cuttings. If your plant has trailing stems with no babies and purple leaves, it is very likely a Tradescantia; other purple trailers such as Gynura (purple passion plant) can also be mistaken for it.

Why is my purple plant turning green?

Your plant is not getting enough light. The purple anthocyanin pigment in Tradescantia pallida (Purple Heart) and Tradescantia zebrina is produced in response to bright light. In low-light conditions, the plant prioritizes chlorophyll production, which causes leaves to turn green. Move to a brighter location and new growth will emerge purple. Spider plant variegation can also fade in low light — move to brighter indirect light for best white coloring.

Can I propagate purple spider plant from cuttings?

If you mean a true spider plant, yes — root baby plantlets in water. If you mean a Purple Heart or Tradescantia, also yes — cut a 4–6 inch stem with 2–3 nodes, remove the bottom leaves, and place in water or moist soil. Both methods root within 1–2 weeks.

Is there a variegated purple spider plant?

No. True variegated spider plants (Chlorophytum) show white or cream stripes — never purple. Plants marketed as variegated purple spider plants are Tradescantia pallida varieties with purple-and-green or striped leaves, or other plants sold under confusing names. Chlorophytum amaniense ‘Fire Flash’ is a real relative of the spider plant, but it has green leaves with orange stalks, not purple foliage. If the leaf base is purple and the stripes are pink or white, you are looking at a Tradescantia, not a spider plant.
