Career decisions are easier when you can see your own patterns: what energises you, what drains you, which skills you use without effort and which goals are really yours. These 75 questions are grouped by stage, from noticing where you are now to planning a specific next step. Use them before a job search, during a performance review, at a crossroads or simply once a month to keep your direction honest.

Quick answer: Pick one section that matches where you are, answer three to five questions in writing, and give each answer at least five minutes. Then read your answers back and circle any idea that appears more than once. Repeated themes are usually your most useful career information.

75 career reflection questions

Where you are now

  1. Which part of your current work would you keep if you could delete everything else?
  2. When did you last lose track of time at work, and what were you doing?
  3. Which tasks do you postpone most often, and why?
  4. What do colleagues regularly ask you for help with?
  5. How would you describe your job to a ten-year-old?
  6. On a scale of one to ten, how much does your work fit your values today? What would raise it by one point?
  7. What did you expect this role to be like, and how is it different?
  8. Which relationships at work make the job better, and which make it harder?
  9. What do you complain about most, and is it something you can change?
  10. How does your work affect your sleep, health and free time?
  11. What have you learned in the last six months that you did not know before?
  12. Which achievement from this year are you quietly proud of?
  13. If you left tomorrow, what would you miss first?
  14. Where do you feel underused?
  15. What would a perfect ordinary Tuesday at work look like?

Skills and strengths

  1. List ten things you can do well. Which three would you enjoy using every day?
  2. Which skill have you developed without anyone asking you to?
  3. What feedback have you heard more than once, good or bad?
  4. Which problems do you solve faster than most people around you?
  5. What would your best manager say is your greatest strength?
  6. Which skill, if you improved it by 20 percent, would open the most doors?
  7. What do you know that would be valuable to someone outside your industry?
  8. Which tools, methods or subjects do you keep reading about in your free time?
  9. When have you taught yourself something difficult, and how did you do it?
  10. Which of your strengths becomes a weakness when it is overused?
  11. What kind of work feels easy to you but seems hard to others?
  12. Which past job or project taught you the most, even if you disliked it?
  13. How do you prefer to learn: by reading, watching, doing or discussing?
  14. What would you need to learn to be ready for your next role?
  15. Which skill do you avoid admitting you lack?

Values and motivation

  1. What matters more to you right now: money, meaning, flexibility, status or security? Rank them.
  2. Which person's career do you admire, and what exactly do you admire about it?
  3. What would you do for work if pay were the same for every job?
  4. When have you felt most respected at work?
  5. Which kind of success would feel empty to you, even if others admired it?
  6. How much risk are you willing to take in the next two years?
  7. What does a good working week look like for your family or personal life?
  8. Which compromises have you made in your career that you would not make again?
  9. Who benefits from your work, and how much do you care about them?
  10. What would you want people to say about your work at your retirement party?
  11. Which work environments bring out your best: quiet or busy, structured or flexible, alone or in a team?
  12. What does 'enough' money mean for you in practical terms?
  13. Which career goal did you inherit from parents, teachers or friends rather than choose yourself?
  14. What are you no longer willing to tolerate in a job?
  15. How important is it that your work is visible to others?

Goals and direction

  1. Where would you like to be working in three years? Describe a single day there.
  2. What is the smallest next step toward that picture?
  3. Which goal have you carried for years without acting on it?
  4. If you could only achieve one professional goal this year, which would it be?
  5. What would you attempt if you knew you could not fail, and what would you attempt if failing were acceptable?
  6. Which roles or industries are you curious about but have never researched?
  7. Who is already doing the work you want to do, and how did they get there?
  8. What would make a career change worth the disruption?
  9. Which goal would you happily drop if no one else knew you had dropped it?
  10. How will you measure progress in the next 90 days?
  11. What does promotion mean to you, and do you actually want it?
  12. Which side project could test a new direction without quitting your job?
  13. What would a sabbatical give you, and what would it cost?
  14. If your career had chapters, what would you call the current one and the next?
  15. What deadline, if any, are you working toward, and is it real?

Decisions and next steps

  1. Write down the career decision you are facing. What are all the options, including ones you have dismissed?
  2. For each option, what is the best case, the worst case and the most likely case?
  3. Which option are you leaning toward, and what evidence supports it?
  4. What information are you missing, and who could give it to you?
  5. Whose advice would you value most on this decision, and what would they probably say?
  6. Which fears are shaping this decision, and how likely are they?
  7. What would you choose if you had to decide in the next ten minutes?
  8. How will you feel about this choice in one year, five years and ten years?
  9. What can you try for a month before committing fully?
  10. Who needs to be part of this decision with you?
  11. Which three conversations do you need to have this month?
  12. What will you stop doing to make room for your next step?
  13. Who could you ask for an informational interview this week?
  14. How will you celebrate when you take the first step?
  15. When will you come back to these answers to check your progress?

How to use these questions well

  1. Write, do not just think. Writing slows you down enough to notice contradictions and patterns.
  2. Use evidence. Answer with specific examples, dates and projects rather than general feelings.
  3. Look for repetition. An idea that appears in three different answers is worth acting on.
  4. Turn insight into an action. End each session with one small, dated next step.
  5. Revisit regularly. Answer the same questions again in three or six months and compare.

Books and tools that go deeper

  • What Color Is Your Parachute? by Richard N. Bolles (first published 1970 and updated many times): a classic guide to identifying transferable skills and job hunting.
  • Designing Your Life by Bill Burnett and Dave Evans (2016): applies design thinking, including small experiments, to career decisions.
  • O*NET OnLine, from the U.S. Department of Labor: free descriptions of hundreds of occupations, including tasks, skills and outlook.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I use career reflection questions?

Monthly works well for most people, with a longer session before reviews, job searches or big decisions. Short, regular reflection builds a record you can compare over time.

Can these questions help with a career change?

Yes. The values, skills and decisions sections are designed for career changes. Combine your answers with research and conversations with people already doing the work you are considering.

Should I share my answers with my manager?

Only what you choose. Some answers, such as your strengths and goals, can help in a development conversation, while others are better kept private.

What if my answers feel contradictory?

That is normal and useful. Contradictions often point to a real trade-off, such as security versus freedom, that you need to decide on consciously.
