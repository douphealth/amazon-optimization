The 14-day reset

  3. Define a minimum version for difficult days.
  4. Track completion, not perfection.

The plan

The 14-day focus and habit reset, day by day

Use this when your routines have slipped and focus feels scattered: after a holiday, illness, a busy launch or a stretch of burnout recovery. It is deliberately small. The goal is one behaviour that runs reliably again, not a complete life overhaul.

DaysWhat to doTime
Day 1Pick one outcome for the next two weeks (for example, "finish the first draft" or "study Spanish most days"). Choose one behaviour that drives it.15 min
Day 2Shrink the behaviour to a two-minute minimum (one paragraph, one flashcard). Write an if-then plan: "After I ___, I will ___."10 min
Day 3Remove the biggest source of friction: phone in another room, the document already open, the book on the desk.10 min
Days 4–6Do the minimum at the cue each day. Tick a tracker. Doing more is allowed but not required.2+ min/day
Day 7First review: was the cue reliable? Was the minimum small enough? Change one thing only.10 min
Days 8–10Add one protected focus block of 25–50 minutes around the behaviour, with notifications off.25–50 min/day
Days 11–13Keep the minimum and the focus block. If you miss a day, restart at the next cue without "making up" for it.25–50 min/day
Day 14Final review: what worked, what failed, what to keep. Decide on one small step up, or repeat the reset.15 min

Why it is built this way

Habits form through repetition of a behaviour in a stable context, and the time to automaticity varies widely: a median of 66 days in one real-world study, with a range of 18 to 254 days (Lally et al., 2010). Fourteen days will not make a habit automatic. It is long enough to restart the repetitions, find the friction and set up a routine you can keep. Linking the behaviour to a specific if-then plan has a well-documented effect on follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006).

What this guide is — and what it is not

This guide is: a short, structured restart for one behaviour — a way to get a slipped routine running again, with a clear cue, a minimum version for difficult days, and reviews on days seven and fourteen.

This guide is not: a habit-formation programme — fourteen days will not make a behaviour automatic — a productivity system, or a substitute for rest or professional support when exhaustion, low mood, or a health issue is the real problem. If a behaviour keeps failing even after you shrink it and change the cue, the problem is usually the plan, the timing, or the workload — not a lack of discipline.

Which reset should you use?

  • Attention feels fragmented at work: use the 7-day focus reset.
  • You keep switching between tasks: start with the single-task block.
  • A routine has slipped and you want it back: use this 14-day reset, with the mini habits and habit stacking guides.
  • You feel exhausted rather than distracted: read burnout vs. stress first. Rest may be the right next step, not a new routine.

Questions people ask

How long does a habit take?

There is no universal number; repetition, context and difficulty all matter.

What happens after a missed day?

Resume at the next opportunity instead of abandoning the system.
