A French Bulldog doesn't need many treats. It needs the right kind, in small amounts, cut to a size it can manage. Below you'll find how much is reasonable, which treats suit a flat face, and which common foods can seriously harm a dog.

How many treats is too many

Treats count toward the day's food, not on top of it. Obesity is the most common nutritional disorder in companion animals, according to a widely cited review in The Journal of Nutrition. Treats are one of the easiest places for extra calories to hide, because they're small and they come often.

Extra weight is linked to orthopedic, heart and lung, and metabolic disease in dogs. A flat-faced breed also carries a higher risk of heat-related illness, so a few spare pounds make hot-weather walks harder.

Working out a treat budget

Start with a daily calorie target from your vet. Age, weight, neuter status and activity all change that number. Then check the calorie statement on the package, which may list calories per treat, per piece or per 100 g.

A common planning figure is about 10% of daily calories for treats. If your vet's target is 600 kcal a day, that leaves 60 kcal for treats and 540 kcal for meals. On days you give extra, take a little off the next meal.

Treats that suit a flat face

Soft and plain works best. Raw carrot, cut into thin coins, is low in calories at about 4 kcal per 10 g, and it breaks into small pieces easily. Peeled cucumber is about 1 kcal per 10 g and mostly water, which makes it a good filler for a dog that likes to crunch.

Plain canned pumpkin is soft and freezes well in small portions. Read the label, though, because pie filling contains sugar and spices. Plain low-fat yogurt works as a training reward at about 6 kcal per 10 g. Some dogs don't handle dairy well, and you should check for xylitol or sweeteners before buying.

Hard-boiled egg, fully cooked and chopped small, adds protein. It's dense at about 15 kcal per 10 g, so a little goes a long way. Fresh fruit can work too: offer seedless apple in small pieces, and keep grapes and raisins away from your dog entirely.

Chewing and choking

French Bulldogs have short faces and narrower airways, so hard or large pieces take more work to chew and swallow. Give small, soft bites, and watch your dog eat. If it gags, wheezes or looks distressed, take the treat away.

Leave out hard biscuits, antlers, hooves, rawhide and cooked bones. They can break teeth, splinter, swell, or lodge in the throat or gut.

Heat is the bigger concern

Treats play a small part in heat trouble. Heat itself is the bigger risk. A 2022 review in Veterinary Evidence found that brachycephalic dogs are at increased risk of heat-related illness. Keep walks short and cool in warm weather, and see our overheating playbook for the warning signs.

Foods to keep out of reach

Xylitol is the one that surprises people most. It appears in some sugar-free gum, some peanut butters and some baked goods. In an eight-dog study, it caused acute liver failure, low blood sugar and clotting problems. Five of the eight dogs were euthanized or died. If your dog has eaten any, contact a vet immediately.

Grapes and raisins can cause kidney injury. A 2022 Dutch study of 95 dogs and 13 cats that ate Vitis fruit found vomiting was the most common sign, and it tracked acute kidney injury. Don't offer them, and contact a vet after any amount is eaten.

Chocolate contains theobromine, which dogs break down slowly. Dark and baking chocolate carry the most risk. Contact a vet if your dog has eaten any.

Onion and garlic, in any form, can damage red blood cells. They also hide in broths, seasonings and some baby foods.

Cooked bones splinter. Rawhide can swell or break into pieces that block the throat or gut. Dog-appropriate chews are the safer choice.

Fatty table scraps can trigger pancreatitis in some dogs. A 2024 review covers the dietary side of managing pancreatitis. Offer plain, trimmed meat occasionally, or skip it.

Raw and freeze-dried treats

Raw and freeze-dried treats look like the natural choice, but they're still raw meat, and raw meat can carry Salmonella and Listeria. A 2019 review of raw diets for dogs and cats describes documented risks, including microbiological hazards.

If you feed them, wash your hands after handling, keep them away from food-prep surfaces, and talk to your vet if anyone in the home has a weakened immune system.

Homemade treats, kept plain

Making your own lets you control what goes in. Keep it plain: no added salt, sugar, spices, onion, garlic or xylitol. Xylitol turns up in some baking mixes sold as healthy, so check the label on anything you buy.

Frozen carrot coins, plain pumpkin frozen in small portions, and small pieces of plain cooked chicken or egg cover most needs. If your dog has allergies, pancreatitis or kidney disease, run any recipe past your vet first.

Training without the extra weight

Training uses a lot of small rewards, so the calories add up fast. Take training treats out of the daily food, break them into pea-sized pieces, and mix in praise and play. Once a behavior is reliable, you can reward it less often.

When to call a vet

Call your vet the same day if your dog vomits, has diarrhea, or seems unusually tired or sore after a new treat.

Get emergency care right away if your dog has eaten xylitol, grapes, raisins or chocolate, collapses, has trouble breathing, or has pale, gray or blue gums. Take the packaging, or a note of what was eaten.

Common questions

Can French Bulldogs eat cheese?

A small amount of plain, low-fat cheese can work as an occasional treat. Some dogs get loose stools from dairy, so start small and watch.

Are carrots a good treat?

Yes, in small pieces. They're low in calories, and you can freeze them. Cut them small enough that your dog can't gulp a whole one.

Can my dog have treats every day?

Yes, as long as they fit the calorie plan your vet set.

Are freeze-dried treats safe?

They can be, but they're still raw meat. Follow the hygiene steps above, and ask your vet before feeding them regularly.

Can puppies have treats?

Yes, in small amounts, counted within the puppy's daily food. Ask your vet how much a growing puppy should have.
