These 75 childhood memory questions help you recover the details of growing up for a journal, a memoir, a family history or a letter to someone you love. They start with the senses, because smells, sounds and places tend to unlock memories more easily than dates, and then move through home, family, school, friendship, play, firsts and the person you were becoming. Use them alone, or ask them of a parent or grandparent and write down the answers.

Quick answer: Start with a sensory question (a smell, a sound, a room), write everything that comes back for ten minutes, and only then move to bigger questions about family and identity. Concrete details such as a wallpaper pattern or a neighbour's name make memory writing vivid and often lead to the stories that matter.

Please note: Some childhood memories are painful. Skip any question that is not right for you, and stop if a memory brings up distress you cannot manage. If you are not safe, contact local emergency services or a qualified support service.

75 childhood memory questions for journaling

Senses and places

  1. What smell takes you straight back to childhood, and where are you when you smell it?
  2. Describe the kitchen of the home you remember best, starting with what was on the table.
  3. What sounds did you hear from your bed at night?
  4. Draw or describe a map of your street, with the names of the neighbours you remember.
  5. What did your favourite hiding place look like from the inside?
  6. Which song, when you hear it now, brings back a particular year?
  7. What was the view from your bedroom window?
  8. Describe a place outdoors where you spent hours: a garden, a field, a car park, a beach.
  9. What did a typical school-day breakfast taste like?
  10. Which piece of furniture or object from your childhood home would you rescue if you could?
  11. What did your home smell like after rain, on a holiday, or on a Sunday?
  12. Describe the texture of something you loved to touch: a blanket, a toy, a pet's fur.

Home and family

  1. Who lived in your home, and where did each person usually sit?
  2. What did a normal weekday evening look like in your house?
  3. What were the unspoken rules everyone in your family followed?
  4. Which family saying or phrase do you still hear in your head?
  5. Describe a family meal you remember clearly. Who cooked, and what was said?
  6. What did your parents or carers do for work, and what did you think their jobs were?
  7. Which relative were you closest to, and what did you do together?
  8. What was a family tradition you loved, and one you secretly disliked?
  9. Describe a time your family laughed together until it hurt.
  10. What did you argue about with siblings, cousins or friends who felt like family?
  11. What was money like in your home: tight, comfortable, never discussed?
  12. Which stories did older relatives tell again and again?
  13. What did you learn by watching the adults around you, rather than by being told?
  14. How did your family celebrate birthdays?

School and learning

  1. Describe your first school building as you remember it from the gate.
  2. Which teacher do you remember most, and why?
  3. What subject did you love, and what did you secretly dread?
  4. Describe a school day that went badly, and how it ended.
  5. What did you bring for lunch, and what did you wish you had brought?
  6. Which book from childhood do you remember reading, or having read to you?
  7. What did you learn outside school that school never covered?
  8. Describe a school trip, performance or sports day in as much detail as you can.
  9. What were you praised for as a child, and what were you told off for?
  10. What did you think you would be when you grew up, and why?

Friends and play

  1. Who was your first best friend, and how did you meet?
  2. What games did you invent, and what were their rules?
  3. Describe a toy you played with until it broke.
  4. What did you and your friends do on long summer days?
  5. Which television programme, film or radio show did you never miss?
  6. Describe a time you got into trouble with a friend.
  7. What did you collect: stickers, stones, cards, stamps?
  8. Who was the most interesting adult in your neighbourhood, and what did you think of them?
  9. What was the best present you ever received as a child?
  10. Describe a friendship that ended, and how you felt about it then.
  11. What was a typical weekend morning like?
  12. Which pet, real or longed-for, mattered most to you?

Firsts and milestones

  1. What is your earliest memory? Write it, then write what you think happened just before it.
  2. When do you first remember feeling truly proud of yourself?
  3. What was your first trip away from home without your family?
  4. Describe the first time you remember being really scared.
  5. When did you first realise adults could be wrong?
  6. What was your first job, chore or way of earning money?
  7. Write about moving house for the first time, or about someone important moving away.
  8. When did you first feel grown up?
  9. What was the first piece of news from the wider world that you remember?
  10. Describe a holiday or journey that changed how you saw the world.
  11. What was the first loss you remember, and who helped you understand it?
  12. Describe your first crush, as honestly as you are comfortable with.

Who you were becoming

  1. What did you believe about yourself at eight that you no longer believe?
  2. What were you most curious about as a child?
  3. When did you feel most like yourself?
  4. What did you hide from the adults in your life, and why?
  5. Which childhood fear stayed with you longest?
  6. What did you dream about doing that you have since done, or still hope to do?
  7. Which qualities from childhood do you still see in yourself today?
  8. What would your childhood self think of your life now?
  9. What do you wish an adult had told you when you were young?
  10. Which memory do you return to when you need comfort?
  11. If you could spend one ordinary afternoon of your childhood again, which would it be?
  12. What would you like the young people in your life to know about your childhood?
  13. Write a short letter to yourself at ten years old.
  14. Which memory from this list surprised you most, and why do you think it came back now?
  15. What story from your childhood do you most want to pass on?

How to use these questions

  • Begin with the senses. Smells, sounds and rooms often unlock memories that direct questions cannot.
  • Write in scenes. Instead of ‘we went to the beach every summer’, write one afternoon at the beach in detail.
  • Keep a list of names. Neighbours, teachers, shops and pets. Names bring back more names.
  • Use old photos and objects. Look at one photograph, then answer a question about what happened just outside the frame.
  • Accept that memory is imperfect. Relatives may remember the same event differently. Note both versions; that difference is often the story.

Turning memories into memoir

If you want to share what you write, choose one memory that still carries feeling and build it into a short scene: where you were, what you saw and heard, what was said, and what you understood only later. Read it aloud. Cut anything that explains the feeling instead of showing it. When writing about living family members, think about how they would feel reading it, and change names if needed.

Frequently asked questions

Why start with sensory questions?

Sensory details such as smells, sounds and rooms are strong memory cues. Starting there tends to bring back more specific scenes than questions about dates or big events.

What if I can't remember much of my childhood?

That is common. Begin with places and objects, look at photographs, or ask a relative a question from the list and write down their answer. Small details often bring back more.

Can I use these questions with a parent or grandparent?

Yes. Many work well as interview questions. Record or write down the answers, and ask follow-ups such as 'What did it smell like?' or 'Who else was there?'

How do I write about difficult memories?

Go slowly, skip questions that are not right for you, and stop if you become distressed. You do not have to write about everything to write honestly.
