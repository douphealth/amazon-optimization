Repotting gives your plant fresh soil and room to grow. Here's exactly how to do it.

🏺 When to Repot

  • Roots growing out of drainage holes
  • Water runs straight through without absorbing
  • Plant looks top-heavy and tips over
  • Growth has slowed despite good care
  • It's been 1-2 years in the same pot
  • Best time: Spring or early summer

📏 Choosing the Right Pot

  • Go up ONE size only (1-2 inches wider diameter)
  • Always use a pot with drainage holes
  • Terracotta is great for plants that like to dry out
  • Plastic retains moisture longer (good for thirsty plants)

🪴 Step-by-Step Repotting

  1. Water the plant 24 hours before repotting (reduces stress)
  2. Prepare new pot: Add a layer of fresh potting mix at the bottom
  3. Remove plant: Tip pot sideways, gently squeeze sides, slide plant out
  4. Inspect roots: Loosen circling roots, trim any dead/brown roots
  5. Place in new pot: Center the plant, top of root ball 1 inch below rim
  6. Add soil: Fill around sides with fresh potting mix, press gently
  7. Water thoroughly: Until water drains from bottom
  8. Wait: Don't fertilize for 2-4 weeks (let roots settle)

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Too big a pot: Excess soil stays wet → root rot
  • No drainage holes: Water pools → root rot
  • Burying the stem: Stem rot, keep at same soil level
  • Fertilizing immediately: Burns stressed roots, wait 2-4 weeks

🧰 What You Actually Need

Repotting needs very little equipment, and most of the money people spend goes on the wrong things. Here is the short list, in order of how much difference it makes.

  • A pot with a drainage hole — non-negotiable. Terracotta breathes and dries fast, which suits snake plants, ZZ plants and anyone who tends to overwater. Plastic or glazed ceramic holds moisture longer, which suits ferns and thirsty tropicals.
  • Fresh potting mix — old soil compacts and stops draining. Do not reuse the mix the plant was struggling in.
  • Perlite — the cheapest upgrade in indoor gardening. Adding roughly one part perlite to three parts mix fixes most drainage problems.
  • Orchid bark — for aroids such as philodendron, monstera and anthurium, which want air around their roots rather than dense soil.
  • Clean pruning snips — for trimming dead or circling roots. Wipe with alcohol between plants so you are not moving disease around.

What to skip: gravel or crocks in the bottom of the pot. It is a persistent myth — a coarse layer actually raises the saturated zone and makes drainage worse, not better.
