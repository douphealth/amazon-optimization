Frequently Asked Questions

Is whey or plant protein better for CrossFit?

Pick the one you digest best and will drink every day. Whey isolate wins on leucine content and speed, so it’s tops right after a brutal WOD. If dairy bloats you or you eat vegan, a blended pea-rice or soy isolate gives you all the amino acids you need—just aim for 25-30 g protein and 2.5 g leucine per shake.

How soon after a WOD should I drink my shake?

Hit your shake within 15 minutes of finishing the WOD; that’s when your muscles soak up carbs and protein fastest for quicker recovery and growth.

Can I use collagen as my only protein source?

No, collagen is incomplete—it lacks tryptophan and has low leucine—so it cannot replace other proteins. Use it as a bonus for joints and skin, but keep eating varied protein foods like dairy, meat, beans, or soy to cover all amino acids.

What is the best low-carb option for keto CrossFit?

The best low-carb fuel for keto CrossFit is 20 g of MCT oil blended into black coffee 30 minutes before training; it gives fast ketones for power without carbs, and you can add a pinch of salt to replace the sodium you lose in sweat.

Do women over 30 need different protein dosing?

Women over 30 can stick to the same 0.8 g per kg body-weight baseline as younger adults, but adding an extra 5-10 g at each meal—about half a scoop of protein powder or a small tub of Greek yogurt—helps counter the muscle loss that speeds up after 35. Aim for 25-30 g of protein at breakfast, lunch, and dinner, and you’ll keep strength and metabolism on track without any complicated math.

Are RTDs worth triple the powder cost?

RTDs save time and taste better, but they rarely beat powder on value; you’re paying for convenience, not extra gains, so stick with powder unless you’re traveling or skipping the shaker daily.

Which additives cause bloating?

Sugar alcohols like sorbitol, xylitol, mannitol, erythritol, and maltitol are the main culprits; they pull water into the gut and ferment quickly, so even small amounts in sugar-free gum, mints, or protein bars can puff you up. Carrageenan (a thickener in plant milks and deli meats) and inulin or chicory-root fiber (added to “high-fiber” snacks) can also trigger gas and bloating in many people.

How do I spot a banned-substance-free brand?

Flip the package and look for the NSF “Certified for Sport” or Informed-Sport logo; both programs buy retail tubs off shelves and run Olympic-lab tests for 250-plus banned drugs, and they publish every lot on live websites. If the brand skips third-party testing or hides a vague “proprietary blend,” leave it on the shelf.
