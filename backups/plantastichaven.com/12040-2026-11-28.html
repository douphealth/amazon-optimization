Quick answer: Philodendron Pink Princess (Philodendron erubescens 'Pink Princess') needs brighter light than most philodendrons, a chunky aroid soil mix that drains fast, and watering only once the top 2–3 cm of soil is dry. Its pink variegation is unstable by nature: give it bright indirect light, prune stems that revert to all-green, and always propagate from a node on a variegated stem. It is toxic to cats and dogs, so keep it out of chewing range.

  • Every cutting needs a node — a leaf alone will never become a new plant.

  • Like all philodendrons, Pink Princess is toxic to cats and dogs.

What makes Pink Princess different from other philodendrons

Pink Princess (Philodendron erubescens 'Pink Princess') is a climbing philodendron with dark green, heart-shaped leaves splashed with bubblegum-pink sectors. The pink is not paint and not a separate pigment layer added on top: it is chimera variegation, meaning the plant carries two genetically different cell lines side by side. Some cells make normal green chlorophyll; others make very little, and those sectors show up pink.

That single fact explains almost everything about caring for this plant:

  • Pink tissue photosynthesizes weakly. A leaf that is half pink has roughly half the working chlorophyll of a green leaf, so the plant needs more light than a green philodendron to make the same energy.
  • Variegation is unstable. New growth can come out more pink, less pink, all green, or occasionally all pink. This is normal biology, not a sign you did something wrong — but it is manageable (see the reversion section below).
  • All-pink leaves are fragile. A leaf with almost no green cannot feed itself. All-pink leaves often yellow and drop sooner than mixed leaves. A healthy plant needs a balance of green and pink across its foliage.

Light: the variegation lever

Light is the one variable that most decides whether your Pink Princess stays pink. In low light, the plant compensates by producing more chlorophyll — which means new leaves emerge greener. In bright indirect light, it can afford to keep producing pink sectors.

Where to put it: Within a metre of a bright east- or west-facing window, or right in front of a north-facing window. A south-facing window works if sheer curtains diffuse the sun. Avoid harsh direct afternoon sun: pink sectors have little chlorophyll to protect them, so they bleach, crisp, and develop dry patches in strong direct light.

Signs to read:

  • Too little light: new leaves come out mostly green, growth slows, stems stretch with long gaps between leaves.
  • Too much direct sun: pink areas turn pale or papery, brown crispy edges appear on the pink sectors first.
  • About right: new leaves keep a mix of pink and green, growth is steady, and leaf spacing stays compact.

Find your light zone

Hold your hand between the plant and the window at midday. A soft, defined shadow means bright indirect light. Almost no shadow means too dark. A harsh, sharp shadow with hot sun on the leaves means direct sun — move it back or add a sheer curtain.

Water: the number-one killer is overwatering

Pink Princess is more often killed by kindness than by neglect. Water only when the top 2–3 cm of soil feels dry to the touch — push a finger in, do not guess from the surface. When you water, soak thoroughly until water runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer. Never let the pot sit in standing water.

Warning signs:

  • Overwatered: yellowing lower leaves, soft or mushy stems, soil that stays wet for days, fungus gnats.
  • Underwatered: drooping leaves that perk up after watering, dry crispy leaf edges, soil pulling away from the pot sides.

Watering frequency depends on light, pot size, and season — in a bright warm room it may need water weekly; in winter, far less. The finger test beats any calendar. If you are unsure, wait a day: this plant tolerates a dry spell far better than wet feet.

Soil and potting: chunky, airy, fast-draining

Standard dense houseplant mix stays wet too long and suffocates roots. Pink Princess wants an aroid mix with large air pockets:

  • 1 part quality potting soil
  • 1 part perlite
  • 1 part orchid bark (plus a handful of horticultural charcoal if you have it)

Always use a pot with drainage holes. Repot only when roots circle the pot or growth stalls despite good light and feeding — typically every 1–2 years — and go up just one pot size. A pot that is too large holds wet soil around a small root system, which is the classic setup for root rot. Our philodendron soil mix guide covers drainage, ingredients, and pH in more detail.

Repot when you see the signals, not on a calendar: roots circling out of the drainage holes, water running straight through without wetting the mix, or growth stalling through a whole growing season despite good light and feeding.

Humidity and temperature

Aim for humidity of 60% or above for best variegation and clean new leaves. Average indoor humidity (40–50%) is survivable, but you will see more brown leaf edges and slower growth. A pebble tray helps slightly; a small humidifier near the plant helps a lot. Grouping plants together also raises local humidity.

Keep temperatures between 18 and 27°C. Avoid cold drafts from windows and doors in winter, and do not place the plant against a hot radiator. Sudden temperature swings stress the plant and can trigger leaf drop.

Support and pruning: give it something to climb

In the wild this is a climber. Given a moss pole or trellis, it grows larger leaves with stronger variegation; left trailing, it stays smaller and leggier. Tie stems loosely to the support as they grow, and keep the moss pole lightly damp so aerial roots can grip it.

Pruning for variegation: when a stem starts producing all-green leaves, prune it back to just above the last leaf that still shows pink. This redirects growth hormones to buds that may carry a better pink-green balance. There is no guarantee — reversion is genetic — but strategic pruning meaningfully improves the odds, and the cuttings you remove can be propagated (see below). Always use clean, sharp snips, and prune in spring when the plant is actively growing.

Propagation: step by step

Propagate only from a stem cutting that includes at least one node — the small bump on the stem where a leaf or aerial root meets it. A leaf without a node will root in water and look alive for months, but it will never become a new plant. For the full method comparison, see our philodendron propagation methods guide.

  1. Choose the right stem. Pick a healthy stem with strong pink variegation across several leaves — not a reverted green stem, and not an all-pink one.
  2. Cut below a node. Using clean sharp snips, cut about 1 cm below a node, keeping 1–2 leaves on the cutting.
  3. Root in your medium. Water (easy to watch), damp sphagnum moss, or perlite all work. Keep the node submerged or buried and the leaves above the medium.
  4. Keep it warm and bright. Bright indirect light, 20–26°C, humidity above 60% if you can manage it. Change water every few days if rooting in water.
  5. Pot up. Once roots are 3–5 cm long, pot into chunky aroid mix. Water-rooted cuttings need a gentler transition: keep the mix evenly moist for the first two weeks, then switch to the normal dry-between-waterings routine.

Reversion rescue: find your fix

Reversion — new growth coming out green — is the most common Pink Princess complaint. Work through the checks below in order; in most cases the first two fix it.

New leaves are greener, but older leaves still show pink

This is almost always a light problem. Move the plant to a brighter spot (still indirect light) and wait for 2–3 new leaves before judging. If the stem keeps producing green leaves after a month in bright light, prune that stem back to just above the last variegated leaf.

An entire stem has gone all-green

Prune the reverted stem back hard, to just above a node that previously produced variegated growth. If no variegated nodes remain on that stem, the stem will stay green — propagate from a different, variegated stem instead, and consider the green stem a healthy (if plain) plant.

New leaves are almost all pink and then die

The plant is producing leaves it cannot feed. This usually follows a sudden light increase or heavy pruning. Give it stable bright indirect light, do not prune further, and let it produce a few balanced leaves. Do not fertilize stressed plants until growth resumes.

Variegation was strong, then faded in winter

Normal seasonal response to lower light. Keep the plant in its brightest spot through winter and avoid repotting or heavy pruning until spring growth returns. Variegation usually strengthens again as light improves.

The first two weeks with a new plant

Most new-plant problems happen in the first fortnight, and almost all of them come from doing too much too soon. When your plant arrives: put it in bright indirect light and leave it there; check the leaf undersides and stems for pests and isolate it from other plants for a few days if you find anything; water only when the finger test says the soil is dry. Do not repot it straight away — a new pot, new soil, and a new home in the same week is three shocks at once. A dropped leaf or two in week one is transplant stress, not failure. Wait two to three weeks before fertilizing, pruning, or moving it to a brighter spot, so you are judging the plant settled, not the plant stressed.

Toxicity: keep it away from pets and children

If a pet chews a leaf

Gently remove any plant material from the mouth, offer water, and call your veterinarian or an animal poison helpline right away — do not wait for symptoms to appear. Keep the plant somewhere pets cannot reach, and pick up fallen leaves promptly.

Like all philodendrons, Pink Princess contains calcium oxalate crystals and is toxic to cats, dogs, and humans if ingested. Chewing typically causes mouth irritation, drooling, and vomiting. Place it on a high shelf or in a room pets do not access, and keep fallen leaves picked up. If a pet eats a significant amount, contact your veterinarian or an animal poison helpline promptly. Buying checklist: how to pick a good one

  • Look at several leaves, not one. A single pink leaf on an otherwise green plant is a warning sign, not a bargain. Choose plants with pink sectors across multiple leaves and stems.
  • Check the stems. Avoid plants where the newest growth is already all-green — that stem has likely reverted.
  • Expect to pay more than a common philodendron. Prices vary widely with variegation quality, plant size, and seller; well-variegated plants command a premium, and suspiciously cheap "pink princess" plants are often reverted or mislabeled.
  • Buy from a seller who shows the actual plant. Stock photos hide reversion. Ask for photos of the exact plant if buying online.

Care routine through the year

Philodendrons do best on a rhythm, not a rescue cycle. This is the whole routine:

  • Weekly: finger-test the soil before watering; turn the pot a quarter turn so growth stays even; glance at leaf undersides for pests.
  • Monthly (spring and summer): feed at half strength; wipe leaves with a damp cloth so dust does not block light; check moss-pole ties and re-tie loosely if stems have grown.
  • Each spring: check whether the pot is root-bound; prune reverted stems; take cuttings while growth is vigorous.
  • Each winter: water less, stop feeding, and move the plant to its brightest spot — then leave it alone until spring.
  • Each summer: water more often as light and warmth increase; watch for pests in warm rooms.
  • Each autumn: reduce feeding and let soil dry a little more between waterings as growth slows.

What variegation really looks like over a year

Even a well-kept Pink Princess will not produce identical leaves all year. Expect streaky months: some leaves heavily pink, some mostly green, occasionally one that is nearly all pink or nearly all green. That variation is the nature of a chimera, not a sign you are doing something wrong. Judge the plant over a season, not a single leaf: if most new growth across several months keeps some pink, it is doing well. Whole stems that go green and stay green are the real signal — that is when pruning and brighter light earn their keep.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my Pink Princess losing its pink color?

Almost always low light. The plant produces more green chlorophyll to compensate, so new leaves come out greener. Move it to brighter indirect light and give it 2–3 new leaves before judging. If a stem stays green, prune it back above the last variegated leaf.

Can I propagate Pink Princess from a single leaf?

No. A leaf without a node will root in water but never grow into a new plant. Every cutting needs at least one node — the bump on the stem where leaves and roots emerge.

How much light does Pink Princess need?

More than a green philodendron: bright indirect light, ideally near an east- or west-facing window. Direct afternoon sun scorches the pink sectors, while dim corners cause reversion to green.

How often should I water Pink Princess?

When the top 2–3 cm of soil feels dry — typically weekly in bright warm conditions, less often in winter. Always use a pot with drainage holes and never let it sit in water.

Is Pink Princess toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. Like all philodendrons it contains calcium oxalate crystals; chewing causes mouth irritation, drooling, and vomiting. Keep it out of pets' reach and contact a veterinarian if a pet eats a significant amount.

Why are the pink parts of the leaves turning brown?

Pink tissue is the most fragile part of the leaf. Brown crispy pink sectors usually mean direct sun or low humidity. Move the plant out of direct sun and raise humidity toward 60%.

Should I cut off all-green leaves?

Prune all-green stems back above the last variegated leaf to encourage variegated regrowth — but do not strip a struggling plant bare. If the plant has few leaves, improve light first and prune once it is growing strongly.

Does Pink Princess need a moss pole?

It does not need one to survive, but it grows larger, better-variegated leaves when it can climb. A moss pole kept lightly damp lets aerial roots grip and supports bigger foliage.

What soil is best for Pink Princess?

A chunky aroid mix: roughly equal parts potting soil, perlite, and orchid bark. The mix must drain fast and hold air around the roots — dense standard potting mix stays wet too long and invites root rot.

Why are new leaves small?

Usually a combination of low light, no climbing support, or hunger. Brighten the light, add a moss pole, and feed at half strength monthly during spring and summer.
