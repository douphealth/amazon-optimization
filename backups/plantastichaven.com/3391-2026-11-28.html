How often to water a philodendron: water when the top 1–2 inches of soil feel dry, then water thoroughly until it drains from the bottom of the pot. In bright light and warm months that is often every 5–7 days for a small plant in a chunky mix; a larger plant in winter may only need water every 10–14 days. Check the soil rather than the calendar, because pot size, light, humidity and the mix all change how fast it dries.

If you water too soon, roots stay wet, oxygen drops, and the plant starts sliding toward rot. If you wait too long, the pot goes bone-dry, leaves droop, and growth stalls. The sweet spot is simple: water thoroughly, then wait until the top layer of soil has dried before watering again.

Quick Answer

Most philodendrons want to be watered deeply, then allowed to partially dry before the next watering. In most homes, that means checking the top 1–2 inches of soil, adjusting for season and light, and avoiding constantly wet potting mix.

Key Takeaways

  • Water philodendrons based on soil dryness, not the calendar.
  • Most varieties do best when the top 1–2 inches of soil dries before watering again.
  • Bright light, warm rooms, and airy soil increase watering frequency.
  • Low light, winter temperatures, and dense mixes slow drying dramatically.
  • Overwatering causes more philodendron deaths indoors than underwatering.

How Often Should You Water a Philodendron?

There is no single perfect schedule, because pot size, humidity, soil mix, root mass, and light levels all change how quickly the pot dries. A small philodendron in chunky mix near bright light may need water every 5–7 days, while a larger plant in winter may only need water every 10–14 days.

Best Way to Check Soil Moisture Before Watering

  • Finger test: If the top 1–2 inches feels dry, it is usually time to water.
  • Lift test: A dry pot feels noticeably lighter than a freshly watered one.
  • Wooden skewer test: Insert a skewer and pull it out; if it comes out damp and dirty, wait longer.
  • Clear nursery pot check: Root visibility makes it much easier to judge moisture accurately.

What Proper Watering Looks Like

  1. Water slowly and evenly around the pot.
  2. Let excess water drain fully from the bottom.
  3. Never leave the pot sitting in stagnant water.
  4. Wait until the mix partially dries again before the next watering.

If your plant is in a dense mix, fix the soil—not just the watering schedule. A better philodendron soil mix for healthy roots will make your watering routine dramatically easier.

Signs You’re Overwatering a Philodendron

  • yellowing lower leaves
  • soft or mushy stems
  • soil that stays wet for too long
  • sour smell from the pot
  • slow growth even during active season

Signs You’re Underwatering a Philodendron

  • drooping leaves that perk up quickly after watering
  • crispy leaf edges
  • dry compacted soil pulling away from the pot
  • slower unfurling of new leaves

How Watering Changes by Season

Philodendrons usually need more frequent watering in spring and summer, when light and temperatures support faster growth. In winter, shorter days and cooler rooms slow water use dramatically. If your home also runs dry from heating, the plant may still lose moisture from leaves even while the soil dries more slowly—so always check the mix instead of guessing.

Cold rooms complicate watering, so it helps to understand how cold philodendrons can tolerate before growth slows or damage begins.

Watering by Philodendron Type

  • Heartleaf and Brasil types: forgiving and easy, but still dislike soggy roots. Philodendron ‘Brasil’ is a variegated form of the heartleaf philodendron (Philodendron hederaceum), so it follows the same rule: water when the top 1–2 inches are dry, and less often in winter or low light.
  • Velvety crawler types: often want more even moisture and warmer conditions.
  • Thicker-leaved cultivars like Philodendron Birkin: can tolerate slightly more drying between waterings.
  • Collectors’ species: usually need closer attention to humidity, root health, and airflow.

Common Watering Mistakes to Avoid

  • watering on the same day every week no matter what
  • using decorative pots with poor drainage
  • assuming drooping always means thirst
  • watering heavily in low light
  • ignoring pests and root health while focusing only on soil moisture

If your plant is already stressed, inspect it for insects too. Many watering problems get confused with infestation, so it helps to review how to identify and treat philodendron pests correctly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do philodendrons like to dry out completely?

No. Most prefer partial drying, not total drought.

Should I mist my philodendron instead of watering?

No. Misting does not replace root-zone watering and usually has only a temporary humidity effect.

What kind of water is best?

Room-temperature water is safest. If your tap water is extremely hard, filtered water may help reduce salt buildup over time.

Can I use self-watering pots?

Sometimes, but only if your mix is airy and you monitor moisture carefully. Philodendrons do not want constantly saturated media.

Final Thoughts

Philodendron watering gets much easier once you focus on conditions instead of schedules. Check the soil, use a chunky mix, water thoroughly, and let the plant tell you what is happening. That simple approach beats almost every “water once a week” rule online.
