Frequently Asked Questions

Can you run a marathon or ultra on a ketogenic diet without losing speed?

Yes, many runners now complete marathons and ultras on a ketogenic or low-carb, high-fat diet without slowing down, but it takes smart planning and a long adaptation phase (often 8–12 weeks). As you become fat-adapted, your body burns more fat at higher intensities, but top-end speed and strong finishes still depend on enough total calories, electrolytes, and sometimes targeted carbs around hard efforts or race day. Blood work, heart-rate data, and test races help you dial in what works for you, and if your pace or recovery drops for more than a few weeks, adjust carbs instead of forcing strict keto.

Is the Keto Diet In Endurance Sports better than a traditional high-carb plan for Ironman triathletes?

The keto diet can help some Ironman triathletes burn more fat and reduce GI issues, but most research up to 2026 shows it often hurts top-end power, race pace, and recovery compared to a well-planned high-carb plan. Long events like Ironman still demand strong carbohydrate availability for hard efforts, climbs, and surges. A smart approach for many athletes is “fuel flexibility”: mostly carbs, with some low-carb or fasted sessions to improve fat use without losing speed. Test any big change in the off-season and track wattage, run pace, and how you feel before using it on race day.

How many grams of carbohydrates can I eat and stay in ketosis while training hard?

Most people who train hard can stay in ketosis with about 20–50 grams of net carbs per day, but some athletes can go up to 60–80 grams and remain in ketosis if they are lean and very active. Start near 20–30 grams, track your blood ketones (aim for 0.5–3.0 mmol/L), and raise carbs in small steps to see your personal limit. Time most of your carbs around workouts, choose whole-food sources, and if ketones drop below your target for several days, cut back slightly.

Is keto or low-carb high-fat safe for female endurance athletes and their hormones?

Keto and strict low-carb, high-fat diets can work short-term for some female endurance athletes, but they often raise stress hormones, disrupt menstrual cycles, lower thyroid function, and reduce bone density, especially with high training loads. Many women do better on a moderate-carb plan (timed around training) that includes whole-food carbs like fruit, potatoes, and rice to support hormones and performance. If you try low-carb, avoid long-term extremes, track your cycle, energy, sleep, and mood, and get bloodwork or a sports dietitian’s input if you see changes.

Can I improve VO2max and high-intensity intervals while following a ketogenic diet?

Yes, you can improve VO2max and high-intensity performance on a ketogenic diet, but it usually takes 4–8 weeks of strict adaptation before your body handles hard efforts well. During this time, keep carbs very low day-to-day, prioritize enough protein (1.6–2.2 g/kg), electrolytes, and total calories, and use structured high-intensity interval sessions 1–3 times per week. Some well-adapted athletes add small targeted carbs (10–25 g before or during intense workouts) without leaving ketosis, which can support peak power without losing fat-adaptation. Track progress with repeats at set power/pace and heart rate, and adjust if you see persistent performance drops, poor recovery, or disrupted sleep.

How long should I stay on keto before deciding if it works for my endurance performance?

Give keto at least 6–8 weeks before judging its impact on your endurance, because your body needs time to fully adapt to using fat as its main fuel. In the first 2–3 weeks, performance often dips; after that, watch for changes in pace, recovery, and how you feel in long sessions. If you see no clear gains, struggle with intensity, or fight constant fatigue after 8–10 weeks (despite good sleep, electrolytes, and calories), it’s likely not the best fit for your performance goals.

What blood ketone level should endurance athletes target for effective nutritional ketosis?

Most endurance athletes perform well in nutritional ketosis with blood ketones between 0.5 and 1.5 mmol/L, measured by a blood meter. Some ultra-endurance athletes may feel best slightly higher, around 1.5–3.0 mmol/L, but levels above that rarely improve performance and can signal under-fueling. Test at consistent times (such as fasted in the morning) and adjust your carb and fat intake based on how you feel, your training load, and race goals.

Should I use exogenous ketone supplements instead of a full ketogenic diet for racing?

Exogenous ketone supplements can give a short-term boost in blood ketones, but they do not replace the full benefits of a well-planned ketogenic diet for racing. Most recent studies show mixed or small performance gains, and some athletes report stomach issues or brain fog at race pace. If you already race well on carbs, focus on dialed-in fueling and hydration first; consider testing ketone supplements only in training, with guidance from a sports dietitian, to see if they work for your body and event.
