Quick answer: Almost every indoor plant problem traces back to one of five root causes — too much water, too little light, a mix that drains too slowly, low humidity, or pests. The symptom on the leaf tells you which. Yellow leaves on damp soil mean root suffocation; crispy brown edges mean moisture is leaving the leaf faster than the roots replace it; mushy tissue means rot is already established. Diagnose the cause before changing anything, because the two most common symptoms — yellowing and drooping — each have an overwatering version and an underwatering version, and the wrong fix accelerates the decline.

How to diagnose an indoor plant problem

Work in this order. Each step rules out a cause, so by the fourth you usually have one candidate left rather than five.

  1. Feel the soil first, not the leaf. Push a finger two inches (5 cm) down. Wet soil with yellow or drooping leaves points to root damage; bone-dry soil with the same symptom points to dehydration. This single check separates the two largest categories of houseplant failure.
  2. Check where the damage sits. Oldest, lowest leaves failing first is usually normal ageing or a watering fault. Newest leaves failing first points to light, nutrient, or root-system problems.
  3. Look at the pattern on the leaf. Uniform yellowing, yellowing between veins, brown edges, and discrete spots are four different diagnoses, not one.
  4. Inspect leaf undersides and stem joints. Most pest infestations are visible there weeks before the top surface shows damage.
  5. Change one variable and wait 7–14 days. Plants respond slowly. Changing water, light, soil, and pot at once means you never learn which one was wrong.

The five root causes behind almost every symptom

Overwatering is the leading killer of indoor plants, and it rarely means watering too often — it means the mix holds water so long that roots sit without oxygen. Insufficient light is second, and it produces stretched stems and shrinking leaves long before anything turns yellow. Low humidity shows up on leaf margins first. Salt accumulation from fertiliser and hard water burns tips over months. Pests are usually the last to be noticed and the easiest to confirm, because you can see them.

Once you have a candidate cause, follow the specific guide below. If two guides seem to apply, treat the watering and light variables first: they change the plant's tolerance for everything else.
