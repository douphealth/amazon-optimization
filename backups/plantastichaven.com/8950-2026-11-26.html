Quick answer: the most reliable orange-flowering houseplants are clivia, orange kalanchoe, goldfish plant, guzmania bromeliad, crown of thorns, orange star and flowering maple. If you have cats or dogs, the genuinely pet-safe options are the goldfish plant, guzmania bromeliad and eternal flame calathea — the rest are toxic if chewed.

🌼 The 7 Best Orange-Flowering Houseplants

1. Clivia (Clivia miniata) — the connoisseur choice

Clusters of orange trumpet flowers on a thick stalk above strap-shaped leaves. It is the most striking plant on this list and the one people most often fail to flower.

The trick to getting blooms: clivia needs a cool, dry rest. Give it six to eight weeks around 10–15°C with almost no water in autumn or early winter. Skip that rest and you get healthy leaves and no flowers, year after year. Do not repot often either — clivia flowers better when slightly pot-bound.

Care: bright indirect light, water when the top third of the mix dries, and never let it sit in water.

Pet warning: all parts contain lycorine. Toxic to cats and dogs.

2. Kalanchoe (Kalanchoe blossfeldiana) — the easiest orange bloom

Dense clusters of small orange flowers over glossy succulent leaves. Supermarket-common, cheap, and blooms for weeks.

The trick to reblooming: kalanchoe is a short-day plant. It sets buds when nights are long, so from autumn give it 14 hours of complete darkness each night for about six weeks. A cupboard or a box over the plant works. Most people throw kalanchoe away after the first flush simply because nobody told them this.

Care: treat it as a succulent. Bright light including some direct sun, water only when the mix is dry through, and use a gritty mix.

Pet warning: contains cardiac glycosides. Toxic to cats and dogs.

3. Goldfish plant (Nematanthus gregarius) — best pet-safe pick

Small pouched orange flowers that genuinely do look like leaping goldfish, on trailing stems of thick glossy leaves. Excellent in a hanging pot.

Care: bright indirect light, evenly moist but never soggy mix, and decent humidity. It flowers on mature growth, so avoid hard pruning if you want blooms.

Pet status: not listed as toxic to cats or dogs. One of the few orange bloomers you can keep at floor level without worrying.

4. Guzmania bromeliad — months of colour, zero fuss

The orange you see is not a flower but a bract, which is exactly why it lasts: three to six months, sometimes longer. The real flowers are small and white, tucked inside.

Care: bright indirect light. Water into the central cup rather than the soil, and flush it out every few weeks so it does not stagnate. Use rainwater or filtered water if your tap water is hard.

Worth knowing: each rosette flowers once, then slowly dies while producing offsets, called pups, around its base. Separate a pup once it is about a third the size of the parent and you have the next plant free.

Pet status: non-toxic to cats and dogs.

5. Crown of thorns (Euphorbia milii) — blooms nearly year-round

Small orange-red bracts on spiny succulent stems. Given enough sun it flowers almost continuously, which nothing else on this list does.

Care: the more direct sun the better — four or more hours. Water sparingly and let the mix dry out fully. It tolerates neglect far better than attention.

Pet and skin warning: the milky sap is a serious irritant to skin and eyes and is toxic to pets. Wear gloves when pruning, and keep this one out of reach.

6. Orange star (Ornithogalum dubium) — brightest orange of all

Star-shaped flowers in an almost fluorescent orange, on a compact stem. Usually sold in bud in spring.

Care: bright light with a little direct sun, moderate water while in growth. It grows from a bulb, so after flowering let the foliage die back naturally, keep it dry for a few months, then resume watering to restart the cycle.

Pet warning: all parts are toxic to cats and dogs.

7. Flowering maple (Abutilon) — hanging lanterns

Papery bell-shaped orange flowers that dangle like little lanterns, on a shrubby plant with maple-shaped leaves. It is not a maple.

Care: the brightest spot you have, including some direct sun, and consistent moisture — it wilts quickly when dry. Pinch the growing tips to keep it bushy rather than leggy, and feed through the growing season.

Pet status: best kept away from pets.

🐾 If You Have Cats or Dogs

Most orange-flowering houseplants are toxic, which is an awkward fact that many round-up articles skip. Orange pigments in this group tend to come with alkaloids or cardiac glycosides.

The safe choices are the goldfish plant, guzmania and other bromeliads, and eternal flame calathea (Calathea crocata), which pushes up orange bracts and is non-toxic. Verify any plant against the ASPCA toxic and non-toxic plant database before bringing it home, and remember that toxic does not always mean fatal — many cases are mouth irritation and vomiting, but that is still a vet visit.

❓ Why Is My Plant Not Flowering?

Not enough light. This is the answer roughly three times out of four. Flowering costs a plant far more energy than making leaves, so a plant in light that is merely adequate will simply keep making leaves. If you cannot comfortably read a book where the plant sits, it is too dark to bloom.

You skipped the trigger. Clivia needs a cool dry rest; kalanchoe needs long nights. Neither will flower on care alone.

Too much nitrogen. General-purpose fertiliser pushes leaf growth at the expense of buds. Switch to a bloom formula with a higher middle number during the flowering season.

Repotted at the wrong moment. Several of these flower better slightly pot-bound. Repotting a clivia often costs you a season.

🧰 What Actually Helps
