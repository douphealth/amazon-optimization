Evidence-led mouse removal

How to Get Rid of Mice

The complete home mouse removal guide: confirm activity, remove food access, trap active routes, clean safely, seal entry points, and know when to call a professional. No poison required, no myths, no guessing.

Quick answer: To get rid of mice, confirm activity in accessible areas, secure food and reduce clutter, set protected mouse-sized snap traps along confirmed routes, safely clean rodent waste, and coordinate durable entry-point repairs with indoor trapping. Check traps daily and monitor for new signs. No fixed number of days proves that a home is mouse-free. Seek professional help for heavy contamination, inaccessible activity, recurring signs or suspected wiring damage. See CDC trapping guidance and UC IPM house mouse management.

Need a plan for your situation? Use the free Mouse Control Planner for a tailored checklist based on your rooms, household and observed signs. It is an educational planning aid, not an infestation diagnosis or a clearance test.

Tonight: three moves. (1) Put all pantry food, pet food, and birdseed into rigid sealed containers. (2) Set snap traps tonight — not at the weekend — along the walls where you saw droppings. (3) Bag and bin anything mice have chewed. Everything else in this guide builds on those three moves.

Find your starting point

Answer one question and jump to the moves that fit your situation. Every branch ends in the same system below — it just starts where you are.

I saw one mouse — what do I do first?

One sighting does not establish the number of mice. Secure food, inspect accessible routes, set mouse-sized traps only where children and pets cannot reach them (see Step 3), and keep the dated record in Step 6. If activity persists, reassess the routes or seek qualified help.

I found droppings but no mouse — where do I start?

Droppings are evidence, not a diagnosis. Their color, texture and appearance cannot reliably establish age or species; do not touch or crush them. See our visual identification guide. Wet-clean accessible waste using Step 5, then monitor the cleaned locations for newly appearing evidence.

I hear noises in the walls or ceiling at night — what now?

Night noises are a clue, not proof of mice or a specific route. Inspect accessible areas for supporting signs. Coordinate trapping and exclusion rather than sealing all exits around active animals. See Mice in Walls or Attic Mice & Insulation for access and safety limits.

I have pets or small children — what is safe?

Follow the trap manufacturer's directions and choose protected placements that children and pets cannot access. A homemade cover is not proof of protection. Avoid loose poison; see the poison section.

I rent — what can I actually do?

Everything in Steps 1–3 and 5–6 is yours to do: food control, trapping, safe cleanup, monitoring. Sealing is the shared part — see the renter playbook for what to do yourself, what to ask your landlord for in writing, and what to do when the unit next door is the source.

Confirm it is mice

Before you trap anything, confirm what you are dealing with. Wrong identification wastes traps and time.

  • Droppings. Mouse droppings are small (about 3–6 mm), dark, and pellet-shaped with pointed ends. Rat droppings are roughly twice as large with blunt ends. Fresh mouse droppings are dark and slightly shiny; old ones turn grey and crumbly. Photos and lookalikes: Mouse Droppings ID Guide.
  • Sounds. Scratching and scurrying inside walls or ceilings at night, especially in the first hours after dark, fits mice. Daytime sounds or heavy thumping point elsewhere.
  • Marks. Greasy smudge lines along baseboards and pipes, gnaw marks on food packaging and wood, and small shredded-nesting piles (paper, fabric, insulation) all corroborate mice. One sign alone is not enough — two or more together is a solid case. Full checklist: Signs of Mice in Your Home.
  • Mice vs rats. If the animal is large, the droppings are blunt-ended and over 12 mm, or you see burrows outside, you may have rats instead. The comparison guide is Rats vs Mice: Key Differences.
  • Deer mouse vs house mouse. Deer mice (white belly, bi-colored tail) carry a higher hantavirus risk than house mice: the most common hantavirus causing hantavirus pulmonary syndrome in the U.S. is spread by the deer mouse (CDC). That changes how carefully you must clean. If you are in a rural or wooded area, treat cleanup with extra care and read the cleanup section before touching anything.

The 7-step mouse removal system

Use these steps as a coordinated process. Prevent new entry while monitoring and trapping mice already inside; avoid blocking active animals into inaccessible cavities.

Step 1: Find where they are active

Traps catch mice only on runways mice actually use. Spend twenty minutes mapping before you set anything:

  • Check the kitchen first (behind the stove and fridge, inside lower cabinets, around the sink pipes), then the pantry, basement, attic hatch, garage, and utility entries.
  • Record visible droppings, marks, damaged food packaging and possible nesting material without touching suspected waste. Use several signs to guide inspection; appearance alone cannot date droppings.
  • Monitor safely accessible locations using a dated observation log and protected traps; do not add dust to areas with suspected rodent contamination.

Step 2: Cut food, water, and shelter tonight

Mice stay where eating is easy. This step alone removes the reason they came:

  • Move pantry goods, pet food, and birdseed into rigid containers (glass or metal — mice chew through plastic bags and thin plastic boxes).
  • Repair dripping taps and remove standing water from leaks and spills. Always provide pets with fresh drinking water.
  • Reduce clutter in safely accessible, uncontaminated areas. If you find suspected rodent waste, follow the wet-cleanup procedure before cleaning; never vacuum or sweep dry waste.
  • Take trash out nightly and keep bins lidded. Outside, move firewood away from the house wall and trim shrubs touching the building.

Step 3: Trap right

Most "traps don't work" stories are placement stories, not trap stories.

  • How many. Match trap numbers to the confirmed activity and accessible routes. UC IPM recommends multiple traps in strategic locations, no more than about 10 feet apart in areas of activity. Follow product instructions and keep every trap away from children and pets.
  • Where. Place traps perpendicular to the wall (trigger end touching the wall) so a mouse running along the baseboard meets the trigger head-on. Set them in pairs a few inches apart on each runway — mice often jump the first trap and hit the second.
  • Bait. A pea-sized dab of peanut butter pressed firmly onto the trigger works better than cheese (mice must work at it, which trips the mechanism). Replace bait every two days; stale bait gets ignored.
  • Traps not working? Recheck the evidence, trap size, position, bait and trigger setup. Follow the manufacturer's instructions. If activity persists despite correctly placed traps, consult a pest-control professional.
  • Checking. Check traps every morning. Remove catches promptly with gloves, reset immediately, and log the catch (see Step 6). A trap left full teaches nothing and smells.
  • Pets and children. Use appropriate protected traps and inaccessible locations according to the manufacturer's directions. Do not rely on an improvised box to prevent access.

Step 4: Seal them out

Durable exclusion helps prevent new mice from entering. Inspect openings and coordinate repairs with indoor trapping and monitoring so that active animals are not trapped in inaccessible voids. Keep required ventilation and building services functional.

Steel wool / copper meshSmall gaps around pipes and wires (mice cannot chew copper mesh; steel wool rusts over time)Large holes on its own — it needs a backing
Hardware cloth (1/4-inch mesh)Vents, larger openings, foundation gapsFine interior cracks (overkill, hard to finish neatly)

Door sweeps / weatherstrippingDoor gaps, garage doors—

Room-by-room gap checklist: kitchen sink and dishwasher pipes; bathroom plumbing; dryer exhaust vent; HVAC and utility entries; attic vents; basement sill plate; garage door edges; any place a pipe or wire enters a wall. A mouse fits through a gap about the width of a pencil — sources vary between a dime's width and about 6 mm, so treat any gap you can fit a pencil into as an entry point.

Foam-alone warning. Expanding foam and caulk alone will not stop mice — they gnaw straight through. Every foam or caulk seal needs a metal backing (mesh or hardware cloth) behind it.

Step 5: Clean up safely

Droppings and nesting material can carry disease, including hantavirus in deer-mouse areas (CDC). Never dry-sweep or dry-vacuum droppings — that puts contaminated dust in the air.

  • For accessible household areas, ventilate for 30 minutes and stay out during ventilation; wear rubber or plastic gloves. Heavy contamination, unsafe access and HVAC involvement need specialist assessment and appropriate protective equipment; ordinary household precautions may not be sufficient. See CDC cleanup guidance.
  • Spray droppings and nesting material with disinfectant until wet, wait the product-label contact time, then wipe up with paper towels and bag them.
  • Disinfect hard surfaces as directed by the product label. For contaminated porous materials, follow the material-specific CDC guidance; arrange qualified assessment for contaminated insulation or extensive waste.
  • Wash hands thoroughly afterward, and launder any cloths used. Full CDC-standard protocol: Rodent Infestation Cleanup Protocols. Safe removal sequence with the same rules: How to Remove Mice Safely.

Step 6: Monitor — and know when you are done

This is the section most guides skip, and it is the difference between "I think they're gone" and "they're gone."

  • Dated record. Keep a simple log: date, trap locations, catches, and fresh-dropping sightings. One line a day is enough.
  • New-evidence checks. Recheck cleaned, accessible locations for newly appearing droppings, gnaw marks or other evidence. A quiet period is useful monitoring information, not proof that a route is inactive or the home is clear.
  • Trap checks. Check traps daily and record catches and new signs. Continue monitoring after activity declines. Empty traps alone cannot establish clearance.
  • Seal check. Verify that exclusion repairs remain intact, continue protected monitoring, and seek a professional assessment when signs persist or access is incomplete.

Step 7: Escalate to qualified professionals when needed

Call a qualified professional for persistent or recurring activity, inaccessible wall or ceiling voids, heavy contamination, HVAC involvement, or suspected wiring damage. Do not wait for a fixed number of weeks if the situation is unsafe. Ask about inspection, exclusion, cleanup scope and follow-up. See the DIY vs pro framework below.

Troubleshooting: traps empty, bait stolen, catches continue

  • Traps empty but signs continue. Reassess safely accessible evidence, trap placement and product instructions. Move traps only to supported routes and keep them out of reach of children and pets.
  • Bait stolen without triggering. Bait was too loose or the trigger too stiff. Press a pea-sized dab of peanut butter hard onto the trigger so the mouse must work at it, and try pre-baiting unset traps for 3–4 days.
  • Only juveniles caught. Juveniles are bolder and less cautious. Adults are likely still present and trap-shy — pre-bait, vary trap positions slightly, and keep going.
  • Catches continue after sealing. You sealed the wrong gaps, or there is a second entry route. Re-inspect with the room-by-room checklist, especially utility entries and the garage.
  • Catches stopped, then resumed. New mice found a new route, or the original entry was never sealed. Treat it as a fresh Step 1 mapping exercise, not a failure of trapping.

What does not work, graded

  • Ultrasonic plug-ins — no. No agency recommends them as a control method, and mice habituate to the sound. They are the most commonly wasted money in mouse control.
  • Peppermint oil and scent repellents — weak and temporary at best. Strong scents may briefly disturb a runway, but they do not remove mice, and the effect fades. They are a supplement, never the plan. Full limits: How to Get Rid of Mice Naturally Without Poison.
  • Mothballs — no, and unsafe indoors. The fumes are a pesticide exposure risk to people and pets, and they do not reliably repel mice.
  • Ammonia, dryer sheets, Irish Spring soap — no evidence. Repeated in blogs, absent from agency guidance. Do not build a plan on them.
  • A cat — unreliable. Some cats hunt mice; many ignore them entirely. A cat is a pet, not pest control.

Poison: the honest section

Most pest companies sell poison. This site does not, so this section can say what they usually will not.

  • Secondary poisoning is real. A poisoned mouse eaten by a pet, owl, hawk, or dog can poison the eater in turn; UC IPM notes that secondary poisoning of pets and wildlife is possible, especially with slow-acting anticoagulant baits. It is the strongest reason not to use rodenticide casually.
  • Dead mice in walls. Poisoned mice often die inside voids you cannot reach. The result is weeks of odor and, sometimes, secondary insect problems — a cost nobody quotes upfront.
  • Children and pets. Loose pellets and poorly secured bait stations are an ingestion risk. Even secured stations are not risk-free if a pet carries a poisoned mouse home.
  • When pros legitimately use it. For severe infestations, professionals sometimes use tamper-resistant bait stations outdoors, as part of a wider plan that includes trapping and exclusion. That is a different risk profile from scattering pellets indoors yourself — and it is the only context in which this guide considers poison defensible.
  • Bottom line. For the large majority of homes, trapping plus exclusion solves the problem faster, cheaper, and with fewer side effects than poison. If a company leads with indoor poison and skips exclusion, get a second quote.

If you hire a professional: what to ask first

  • Licence and insurance: confirm any legally required pest-control licence, and ask what the insurance actually covers. Neither proves results.
  • A named route and plan: the inspector should describe the entry routes they found, how they will reach them, the sealing (exclusion) plan, and how results will be monitored.
  • Methods used indoors: ask which traps or baits they will use inside. With children or pets, ask for no loose indoor poison.
  • Follow-up and exclusions: get the number of follow-up visits, what is excluded, and any re-treatment terms in writing.
  • References: ask for recent local references rather than relying on a “guaranteed” claim.

Renter and apartment playbook

No other guide covers this properly, and renters are a large share of mouse sufferers. Shared walls change the game.

  • What you can do yourself. Everything in Steps 1–3 and 5–6: food control, trapping, safe cleanup, monitoring. These need no landlord permission and no building modification. See also the dedicated apartment mouse removal plan.
  • What to ask your landlord for, in writing. Sealing of structural gaps (sill plate, utility entries, shared-wall penetrations), pest treatment of common areas, and repair of conditions that attract mice (leaks, trash storage). Put the request in writing with dates and photos of evidence — written requests create a record that verbal ones do not.
  • When the unit next door is the source. Trapping your unit alone will not hold if mice breed next door. Document the pattern (catches resume after clearing, droppings concentrated on the shared wall), share it with your landlord in writing, and ask for a building-wide inspection. If the landlord does not act, check your local tenant-rights rules — many jurisdictions require landlords to maintain pest-free habitability.
  • What not to do. Do not seal shared-wall penetrations that carry building services you do not understand, and do not use poison in a shared building — a poisoned mouse can die in a neighbor's wall, and bait can reach neighbors' pets.

Keep them out: prevention and the fall checklist

Mice move indoors most in autumn, when temperatures drop and food outside thins. A one-hour fall pass prevents most winter infestations:

  • Walk the exterior with a flashlight: check the sill plate, foundation vents, dryer exhaust, and every pipe or wire entry. Seal what you find with mesh plus caulk (never foam alone).
  • Move firewood at least 20 feet from the house wall and off the ground; trim shrubs and tree branches touching the building.
  • Secure trash with tight lids; clean up fallen fruit and birdseed around the yard.
  • Inside: pantry goods into rigid containers before the first cold snap, not after the first sighting.
  • Set two or three traps in the usual entry rooms as tripwires through the first cold month, even with no sightings. An empty tripwire trap is information, not waste.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to get rid of mice?

Secure food and place suitable snap traps along confirmed, safely accessible routes tonight, following the manufacturer's instructions. Check traps daily. Results vary; lasting control also requires durable exclusion and monitoring.

How many traps do I need — and if I see one mouse, how many are there really?

The number depends on the observed routes and safe placement options. UC IPM advises multiple traps in active areas rather than a fixed number for every home. A single sighting does not reveal the total number of mice.

Does peppermint oil get rid of mice?

No, not as a control method. It may briefly disturb a runway, but it does not remove mice and the effect fades. Treat it as a supplement at most, never the plan.

Do ultrasonic pest repellers actually work?

No reliable evidence supports them, and no agency recommends them for mouse control. Mice habituate to the sound. Spend the money on traps and mesh instead.

Will mice leave on their own?

Almost never. A warm home with food and shelter is ideal habitat. Without trapping and exclusion, the population grows — extension sources commonly cite 5–10 litters a year, around 5–6 pups each, which is why "wait and see" fails.

How are mice getting into my house?

Through gaps about the width of a pencil: pipe and wire entries, dryer vents, foundation cracks, door gaps, and the sill plate. Walk the room-by-room checklist in Step 4 — the gap is usually somewhere you have never looked.

Is it safe to clean up mouse droppings — what diseases do mice carry?

Wet cleanup, ventilation and gloves reduce exposure during suitable household cleanup; they do not eliminate all risk. Do not dry-sweep or vacuum rodent waste. Heavy or inaccessible contamination requires additional precautions and qualified help. See diseases from mice and the safe-cleanup procedure.

How long does it take to get rid of mice?

There is no reliable universal removal timeline. Daily trap checks and the absence of newly appearing evidence are useful monitoring signals, but neither proves clearance. If activity continues or important areas are inaccessible, arrange a professional inspection.

Should I use poison — is it safe with pets and kids in the house?

No loose poison indoors with pets or children, period. Secondary poisoning and ingestion risks are real, and poisoned mice die in walls. Trap and exclude instead; if a professional suggests poison, ask for a no-indoor-poison plan first.

When should I call an exterminator, and what does it cost?

Call when signs persist despite appropriate control, when activity is inaccessible or when contamination or property hazards make DIY unsafe. Request itemized local quotes for inspection, trapping, exclusion, cleanup and follow-up.
