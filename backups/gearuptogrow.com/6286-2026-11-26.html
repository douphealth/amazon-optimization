  5. Spaced Repetition: A Simple Review Schedule for Long-Term Memory

Quick answer: Spaced repetition means reviewing information after increasing delays so you revisit it before forgetting takes over. A simple beginner schedule is same day, next day, three days later, one week later, two weeks later, and monthly. It works best when each review uses active recall, not passive rereading.

Best for: students, self-learners, certification candidates, language learners, and anyone trying to remember information beyond the next test.

Use this when: you forget material after rereading, cram before exams, or want a simple review calendar that works with flashcards, notes, practice problems, and teach-back prompts.

Key Takeaways

  • Spaced repetition is a scheduling system for memory, not a magic app.
  • The review should get harder before it gets easier: try to recall before checking the answer.
  • Review fewer high-value questions instead of rereading entire chapters repeatedly.
  • Pair spaced repetition with active recall for stronger learning.

How Spaced Repetition Works

Spaced repetition works by spreading review over time. Instead of studying the same material repeatedly in one sitting, you revisit it after delays. Each successful recall tells you the material can wait longer. Each miss tells you the material needs a shorter interval or a better question.

The practical reason it works is simple: memory is strengthened when you retrieve information, correct errors, and return later before the concept disappears completely. This is why spaced repetition should be active. Looking at notes and thinking “I know this” is not the same as proving you can retrieve it.

The Science: Why Spacing Works

Spaced repetition isn't folk wisdom — it's one of the most replicated findings in learning science:

  • Ebbinghaus (1885) first mapped the forgetting curve: recall drops steeply within hours unless the material is revisited. Each spaced revisit flattens the curve.
  • Cepeda et al. (2006) synthesized 254 studies on distributed practice and confirmed the spacing effect across subjects, ages, and retention intervals. Longer gaps (up to a point) produce stronger long-term retention than massed study.
  • Dunlosky et al. (2013), reviewing ten learning techniques for the Association for Psychological Science, rated distributed practice and practice testing as the only two strategies with high utility — above rereading, highlighting, and summarization, which all rated low.
  • Roediger & Karpicke (2006) showed that retrieving information (testing yourself) strengthens memory far more than restudying — the "testing effect" that makes each spaced review an active-recall event, not passive rereading.

The practical takeaway: spacing tells you when to review; retrieval tells you how. The schedule on this page implements the timing; the planner below implements it for your actual subjects.

A Simple Spaced Repetition Schedule

Use this schedule as a starting point, not a universal rule. If recall is difficult, review sooner and correct the error; if recall is consistently easy, extend the interval. The right spacing depends on the material, your prior knowledge, and your deadline.

Review When Goal What to do
1 Same day Encode the structure Recall the main ideas without notes, then correct gaps
2 Next day Find early gaps Answer your hardest questions or redo missed problems
3 3–4 days later Strengthen weak items Review only weak items and common mistakes
4 1 week later Transfer to use Use practice problems, teach-back, or blank-page recall
5 2 weeks later Build flexibility Mix topics so you must choose the right method
6 Monthly Maintain Refresh high-value concepts, formulas, definitions, and decision rules

This schedule is intentionally simple. Advanced apps can personalize intervals, but a beginner does not need complexity before consistency.

Long-term track vs 30-day exam sprint

If you have a fixed exam date, compress the gaps so your last review lands just before the exam. If you want to keep the material for months, let the gaps keep growing. Both columns are example schedules to adapt, not tested optimums.

Session Long-term track 30-day exam sprint
First study Day 0 Day 0
Review 1 Day 1 Day 1
Review 2 Day 3 Day 3
Review 3 Day 7 Day 6
Review 4 Day 16 Day 12
Review 5 Day 35 Day 24
Review 6 Day 75 Day 30: practice exam under test conditions

Why the two tracks differ: in a large study of spacing, the gap that produced the best recall grew with how long people needed to remember the material, so a short deadline calls for shorter gaps (Cepeda et al., 2008, Psychological Science). Miss a review? Do it as soon as you can and count the next gap from that day.

Spaced Repetition vs Cramming

Spaced repetitionCramming (massed practice)
Retention after 1 weekHigh — each review rebuilds the trace before it fadesLow — the trace was never consolidated
Retention after 1 monthDurable with monthly refreshMostly gone
Daily time costShort sessions spread over weeksMarathon sessions before the test
StressLow — the work is already doneHigh — everything depends on one night
Transfer to new problemsBetter — interleaved reviews build flexible knowledgeWeaker — recognition without recall

Cramming can work for a test tomorrow. For anything you need to remember next month — a final exam, a certification, a language — spacing wins on every measure researchers track.

How Anki's Algorithm Works (SM-2)

Apps like Anki automate spacing with the SM-2 algorithm (Wozniak, 1987). The logic is simple enough to do on paper:

  1. Every card has an easiness factor (starts at 2.5) and an interval (starts at 1 day).
  2. When you review, you grade yourself: again, hard, good, or easy.
  3. Good multiplies the interval by the easiness factor (1 day → 2.5 → ~6 days → ~15 days…). Easy stretches it further; hard shrinks the factor; again resets the interval to 1 day.
  4. Over weeks, easy cards drift to months-long intervals while hard cards stay on short loops — the schedule personalizes itself.

The 1-3-7-14-30 schedule on this page is a fixed-interval approximation of the same idea. It won't adapt per card like SM-2, but it needs no app and works on paper — which is why the planner above uses it.

What Should Go Into Spaced Repetition?

  • Definitions that must be precise.
  • Formulas, vocabulary, dates, facts, and terms.
  • Common mistakes and exceptions.
  • Decision rules you need to use automatically.
  • Practice problem types, not only answers.
  • Concept explanations you need to teach in your own words.

How to Design Better Review Cards

Good review cards are small, clear, and testable. Bad cards are too broad, too vague, or too easy to recognize without retrieval. A useful card usually has one question, one concise answer, one example, and one common mistake.

Weak card Better card Why
Explain photosynthesis What are the inputs and outputs of photosynthesis? Specific and gradable
Chapter 3 notes What were the three causes of X in Chapter 3? Tests a concrete answer
Remember this formula When should I use this formula, and what does each variable mean? Tests decision-making, not only memory
Vocabulary list Use this word in one original sentence Requires application
Definition only Definition + one example + one common mistake Prevents isolated memory

Spaced repetition is only as good as the questions inside it. A smaller deck of clear, useful cards beats a huge deck of vague prompts.

Spaced Repetition Examples

Subject What to review Best review style
Language learning Vocabulary, phrases, grammar patterns Flashcards plus original sentences — never isolated words only
Math Formulas, problem types, mistakes Practice problems and error review
Medicine / biology Processes, terms, mechanisms Question cards and diagram recall
Law / policy Rules, exceptions, cases Scenario questions and issue spotting
Business / strategy Frameworks, decision rules Teach-back and “when should I use this?” prompts

Exam student: Turn lecture topics into questions the same day, review misses the next day, then run practice questions before the exam rather than rereading chapters.

Technical learner: Space formulas, concepts, commands, and error patterns. Use problems, not just flashcards, for material that requires application.

Professional learner: Create decision cards — “When should I use this framework?” and “What mistake should I avoid?” That makes learning practical at work.

Why Spaced Repetition Needs Active Recall

Spaced repetition tells you when to review. Active recall tells you how to review. Without active recall, spaced repetition can become scheduled rereading. That feels organized, but it does not prove you can retrieve the material when it matters.

Use this loop:

  1. See the question or prompt.
  2. Try to answer from memory before looking.
  3. Check the answer.
  4. Mark easy, shaky, or missed.
  5. Schedule the next review based on difficulty.

App vs Notebook: Which Should You Use?

Option Best for Watch out for
Flashcard app Large sets, languages, exams, adaptive review Collecting too many cards and never pruning
Notebook calendar Small courses, simple systems, low-tech learners Forgetting to schedule the next review
Spreadsheet Self-learners tracking topics and dates Overbuilding the tracker instead of studying
Practice book Math, coding, exams, technical skills Reviewing answers instead of solving again

The best tool is the one you will actually use. A simple calendar beats a perfect app abandoned after four days.

Common Mistakes

  • Reviewing too much passive material instead of high-value questions.
  • Making cards that are too broad to answer clearly.
  • Skipping correction after failed recall.
  • Adding new cards faster than you review old ones.
  • Using the app as a productivity toy instead of a memory system.
  • Ignoring practice problems for subjects that require application.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is spaced repetition only for flashcards?

No. Flashcards are common, but the same principle works for practice problems, summaries, diagrams, teach-back prompts, and review calendars.

What is the best spaced repetition schedule?

Start with same day, next day, 3–4 days later, one week later, two weeks later, and monthly. Adjust intervals based on how well you recall.

Should I reread before reviewing?

Usually no. Try to recall first, then check the source. The attempt exposes gaps and makes correction more useful.

Can I use spaced repetition without an app?

Yes. Use a notebook, calendar, spreadsheet, or folder system. The schedule matters more than the software.

What should I do when I miss a card?

Shorten the interval, improve the question, add an example, and test again soon. A miss is data, not failure.

How does spaced repetition connect to active recall?

Spaced repetition schedules the review; active recall makes the review effective by forcing retrieval before checking the answer.

How often should I review flashcards?

Review each card right before you would forget it. For new material that usually means same day, next day, three days later, one week later, two weeks later, then monthly. Well-known cards can move to longer gaps; cards you keep missing should return to daily review.

How does the Leitner box system work?

The Leitner system uses five physical boxes reviewed on expanding intervals (for example daily, every 2 days, every 4, every 8, every 16). A correct answer moves a card to the next box; a wrong or hesitant answer sends it back to box one. It is a simple way to run spaced repetition with paper cards instead of an app.
