Plant comparison

Short answer: Maranta and Calathea are different genera in the Marantaceae family, although both are commonly called prayer plants. Maranta usually grows lower and spreads, while Calathea and related Goeppertia plants more often form an upright clump. The better choice depends on the exact species, available light, humidity, water quality, and how consistently the plant can be cared for.

Identification cue: Start with growth habit and leaf structure rather than color alone. A low, spreading plant with prominent vein markings is more likely to be Maranta; an upright clump with a broad range of leaf shapes may be Calathea or Goeppertia. Nursery labels can be inconsistent, so compare several cues before deciding.

Are Maranta and Calathea the same plant?

No. They belong to the same botanical family but are separate genera. The shared “prayer plant” label is a common-name shortcut, not proof that two plants are the same. It is also why a plant may be sold under an older Calathea name even when a current classification places it in Goeppertia.

How to choose between Maranta and Calathea

  • Choose Maranta when you want a lower, spreading shape. It can work well on a shelf or in a hanging position when the available light is bright but filtered.
  • Choose Calathea or Goeppertia when you prefer an upright foliage clump. Check the mature size and the exact species rather than relying on the broad genus name.
  • Choose based on your room, not a universal “easy” label. Water quality, dry air, drafts, light, pot size, and watering consistency can matter more than the genus alone.

Do Maranta and Calathea need the same care?

They can share a similar starting approach, but they are not interchangeable care instructions. Use bright, indirect light and a container with drainage. Choose a potting mix that holds some moisture while still allowing excess water to leave, then adjust watering to the plant’s drying rate, pot, light, and room conditions. Avoid keeping the root zone constantly saturated.

Humidity can help both groups, especially in a dry room, but a single target percentage does not fit every home or species. As a rule of thumb, Maranta tolerates average indoor humidity better, while Calathea and Goeppertia usually look their best with more consistent moisture in the air. Water quality is where the two most often diverge in practice: Calathea is more prone to brown, crispy leaf edges from fluoride, chlorine, or dissolved minerals in tap water, so many growers use filtered, rain, or distilled water for it. Maranta is usually less reactive, though very hard water can still cause tip burn over time. Watch the plant and the medium: crispy edges, persistent wetness, faded growth, or leaf loss can have several causes, including light, roots, water quality, pests, temperature, or watering technique.

Common identification mistakes

  • Using “prayer plant” as a botanical name: it is a common label used for several related plants.
  • Relying on color alone: patterned leaves can look different under nursery, home, and camera lighting.
  • Ignoring growth habit: spreading stems versus an upright clump are useful clues when combined with leaf shape.
  • Treating a cultivar label as a species diagnosis: confirm the plant’s current name when the exact care or safety question depends on it.

Maranta vs Calathea FAQ

Is a Maranta a Calathea?

No. Maranta and Calathea are separate genera in the Marantaceae family. They are closely related and can look similar, which explains the common-name confusion.

Do both plants fold their leaves?

Many plants sold as prayer plants show movement between day and night, but the amount of movement varies. Leaf movement is useful context, not a stand-alone identification test.

Which is easier for beginners?

Maranta is generally the more forgiving choice. It tolerates average home humidity and short lapses in care better, and it recovers more readily from occasional neglect. Calathea and Goeppertia tend to be more particular, showing crispy leaf edges sooner when humidity is low or tap-water minerals build up. If you want the lower-maintenance prayer plant, start with Maranta; choose Calathea when you can keep conditions steady. A well-matched plant in suitable light and a draining pot will still beat a poorly matched one regardless of the label.

Which one needs more humidity?

Both can benefit from protection from very dry air, but the exact need varies by species and environment. Improve the growing conditions together—light, watering, airflow, temperature, and humidity—instead of treating humidity as the only variable.
