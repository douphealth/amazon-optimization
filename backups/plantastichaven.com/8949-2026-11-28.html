The short version: what to buy first

Most houseplants die from watering mistakes, not from missing equipment. If you buy nothing else, buy a moisture meter and a pot with drainage. Everything below is ranked by impact, with typical street prices so you can budget.

The core six: buy these first

1. Soil moisture meter

$8–$15Everyone

The single highest-impact purchase for a beginner. Overwatering is the most common cause of houseplant death, and “the top inch feels dry” is unreliable in deep or dense pots.

  • Analogue probe types need no batteries and are accurate enough
  • Probe long enough to reach the root zone, not just surface soil
  • Single-function moisture meters beat 3-in-1 combos
Skip: 3-in-1 meters claiming to read pH and light too — the pH function is unreliable in potting mix.

2. Pots with drainage holes

$8–$25Everyone

A pot without drainage will eventually kill the plant in it, no matter how careful you are. Material matters less than the hole.

  • Terracotta breathes and dries fast — best for succulents, snake plants, ZZ plants, and chronic overwaterers
  • Plastic or glazed ceramic holds moisture — better for ferns, calatheas, and thirsty tropicals
  • Size up by one to two inches of diameter only
Skip: decorative pots with no hole, unless you keep the plant in a plastic nursery pot inside them.
Browse pots with drainage → Read first: how to repot houseplants → Deep dive: terracotta pots for indoor plants →

3. Potting mix and amendments

$8–$15Everyone

Bagged all-purpose soil is usually too dense for indoor aroids. Most people get better results amending a decent base mix than buying a specialist blend.

  • Perlite for drainage — the cheapest upgrade you can make
  • Orchid bark for chunky aroid mixes (philodendron, monstera, anthurium)
  • Coco coir to hold moisture without compacting like peat
Skip: garden soil from outdoors — it compacts in pots and can bring pests indoors.
Browse perlite & amendments → Read first: indoor plant care basics → Recipe: the philodendron soil mix we use → Recipe: snake plant soil mix →

4. Pest control kit

$15–$30Everyone, eventually

Every plant owner deals with pests eventually. Having treatment on hand before an outbreak is the difference between losing one leaf and losing the plant.

  • Neem oil — broad spectrum, works on spider mites, aphids and scale
  • Insecticidal soap — faster knockdown, gentler on tender foliage
  • Yellow sticky traps — the only reliable fix for adult fungus gnats
  • 70% isopropyl alcohol — for spot-treating mealybugs with a cotton swab
Skip: systemic granules in homes with pets or small children.
Browse neem oil & pest control → Read first: houseplant pest control → Organic pest control for houseplants →

5. Grow lights

$20–$60Low-light homes only

Worth buying only if you actually have a light problem. Test first: if you cannot comfortably read a book at the plant position during the day, the light is too low.

  • Full-spectrum LED — ignore purple panels for living spaces
  • Look for a stated PPFD or true wattage, not equivalent marketing numbers
  • A timer matters more than raw power — aim for 10 to 12 hours
Skip: tiny clip-on ring lights — they rarely deliver usable intensity beyond a few inches.
Browse grow lights → Read first: plant light requirements → Pair with: the best low-light houseplants →

6. Humidity: hygrometer before humidifier

$8–$15Tropical plant owners

Buy the cheap measuring tool before the expensive fixing tool. Many homes blamed for low humidity are fine, and the real problem turns out to be watering or light.

  • A digital hygrometer costs little and settles the question
  • Most tropical houseplants are comfortable at 40 to 60% relative humidity
  • If you do need a humidifier, cool-mist and easy to clean beats large capacity
Skip: misting bottles as a humidity strategy — the effect lasts minutes and can encourage fungal leaf spotting.

Worth adding once you have the basics

None of these are urgent. But once your watering and potting setup works, they make care easier and your plants noticeably better.

7. Long-spout watering can

$15–$303+ plants

A narrow spout delivers water to the soil, not over the leaves and crown. Keeping water off the crown matters for snake plants, succulents and African violets, and it keeps water off leaves that spot easily.

  • 1–2 litre capacity is enough for most homes
  • A long, thin spout for reaching into dense foliage
  • Removable rose (sprinkler head) for gentle watering of seedlings
Skip: huge cans you cannot lift comfortably when full — you will water less often because of it.
Browse watering cans → How often to water indoor plants →

8. Balanced liquid fertiliser

$10–$15Growing plants

Potting mix runs out of nutrients within a few months. A balanced, water-soluble fertiliser (such as a 10-10-10 or 20-20-20 diluted to half strength) fed during active growth in spring and summer keeps growth steady.

  • Water-soluble liquid or powder you dilute yourself
  • Feed only during spring and summer, not in winter
  • Half the label strength is plenty for houseplants
Skip: fertiliser spikes — they concentrate salts in one spot of the rootball and can burn roots.
Browse liquid fertilisers → Fertilising philodendrons for optimal growth →

9. Pruning shears and a repotting trowel

$10–$25Repotting & pruning

Clean cuts heal fast; crushed stems invite rot and disease. A sharp pair of bypass pruners or snips, plus a small trowel for moving mix, covers almost every job a houseplant owner faces.

  • Bypass pruners or sharp snips — not kitchen scissors
  • Wipe blades with 70% isopropyl alcohol between plants
  • A narrow trowel for filling gaps when repotting
Skip: anvil-style pruners, which crush rather than cut soft stems.
Browse pruning tools → How to repot houseplants → Pruning and shaping philodendrons →

10. Propagation station

$12–$25Pothos, philodendron, monstera

Every pruning session produces cuttings, and cuttings root in plain water on a windowsill. A propagation station or a few glass vases turns trimmings into free new plants — and lets you watch roots form so you pot them up at the right moment.

  • Clear glass so you can see root development
  • Narrow necks or insert holes to hold cuttings upright
  • Change the water weekly to keep it oxygenated
Skip: opaque containers — you want to see when roots reach 5–10 cm before transplanting.
Browse propagation stations → Read first: the plant propagation guide → Rooting philodendron cuttings in water →

11. Moss poles and plant supports

$10–$20Climbing plants

Monstera and vining philodendrons are climbers. Given a pole to attach to, they grow larger, more fenestrated leaves; left to trail, they stay smaller. A moss pole or coir stake plus a few soft ties is the whole upgrade.

  • Sphagnum moss poles hold moisture and encourage aerial roots to attach
  • Extendable poles save repotting later as the plant grows
  • Use soft ties or clips — never wire directly on stems
Skip: stakes for plants that are not climbers — a snake plant or pothos does not need one.
Browse moss poles & supports → How to stake a philodendron → Why your monstera is not splitting →

12. Cleaning and maintenance kit

$5–$15Nice to have

Dust blocks the light your leaves are trying to catch. A soft microfibre cloth, a gentle shower or wipe-down every few weeks, and a dedicated spray bottle for pest treatments keep plants photosynthesising and problems visible.

  • Microfibre cloths — no leaf-shine products, which clog pores
  • A labelled spray bottle reserved for neem or soap treatments
  • A soft brush for fuzzy-leaved plants that hate wet leaves
Skip: commercial leaf-shine sprays — they coat leaves and block gas exchange.

Build your kit by budget

The ~$30 fix (do this first)

Moisture meter (~$10) + a bag of perlite (~$10) + one terracotta pot with drainage (~$10). This combination prevents the two most common ways houseplants die: overwatering and suffocating soil. If your plant is currently in a pot with no hole, repot it into one that has a hole this week.

The ~$60–$80 problem-solver kit

Everything above, plus the pest kit (neem oil, insecticidal soap, yellow sticky traps) and a digital hygrometer. Now you can measure, water, feed and treat — which covers roughly 90% of the problems covered in our plant problem diagnosis guide.

The ~$90–$140 complete setup

Add a full-spectrum LED grow light with a timer (only if your rooms are genuinely dark), a long-spout watering can, liquid fertiliser, and pruning snips. This is the full working kit of an experienced houseplant owner — nothing more is needed for the plants in our best indoor plants guide.

Match tools to your plants

Different plants fail in different ways. Buy for the plants you actually own:

  • Succulents, snake plants, ZZ plants: terracotta pot + extra perlite or gritty mix. See our snake plant soil mix and succulent soil mix recipes.
  • Aroids (monstera, philodendron, anthurium): chunky bark-based mix, a moss pole, and a pot one size up. Start with the philodendron soil mix and monstera care guide.
  • Ferns, calatheas, marantas: plastic or glazed pot that holds moisture, a hygrometer, and filtered or rain water if your tap water is hard. See maranta vs calathea.
  • Plants you are propagating: glass vases or a propagation station, sharp snips, and fresh mix for potting up. Follow the propagation guide.
  • Pet households: check toxicity before buying plants, and keep pest treatments pet-safe. See pet-friendly houseplants and pothos toxicity.

What we deliberately do not recommend

Self-watering globes, which empty unpredictably and manage to cause both drought and rot. Decorative pots with no drainage hole. Fertiliser spikes, which concentrate salts in one spot in the rootball. Leaf-shine sprays, which clog leaf pores. And anything marketed as a cure-all — if a product promises to replace paying attention to your plant, it will not.

Frequently asked questions

What tools do I need for houseplants as a beginner?

Two things matter more than everything else combined: a soil moisture meter ($8–$15) and a pot with a drainage hole. Most houseplants die from watering mistakes, and these two items directly prevent them. Add perlite to open up dense potting mix, and you have a complete beginner setup for around $30.

Are cheap soil moisture meters accurate enough?

Yes, for houseplant purposes. A basic analogue probe meter tells you whether the root zone is wet, moist or dry, which is the only information you need. Avoid 3-in-1 meters that claim to also read pH and light — their pH readings are unreliable in potting mix.

What should I buy when I bring home a new houseplant?

Check the pot first: if it has no drainage hole, buy one that does (one to two inches wider than the nursery pot) and repot within the first week. Keep the plant in its nursery pot while you watch it settle for a few days if you prefer. A moisture meter is the only other immediate purchase.

Do houseplants need a humidifier?

Usually not. Most tropical houseplants are comfortable at 40–60% relative humidity, which many homes already provide. Buy a digital hygrometer first ($8–$15) and measure. If humidity is genuinely low, a cool-mist humidifier that is easy to clean beats a large-capacity one. Misting is not a substitute — the effect lasts minutes.

Do I need a grow light?

Only if the room is genuinely dark. Do the reading test: if you cannot comfortably read a book at the plant’s position during the day, light is too low. If you do buy one, choose a full-spectrum LED with stated PPFD or true wattage, and run it on a timer for 10–12 hours a day.

Are self-watering globes worth buying?

No. They empty unpredictably — sometimes too fast, sometimes not at all — and can cause both drought stress and root rot. A moisture meter and a consistent weekly check-in is more reliable and cheaper.

How do I get rid of fungus gnats?

Yellow sticky traps catch the flying adults, but the larvae live in the soil. Let the top layer of mix dry out between waterings (a moisture meter helps), and consider repotting into fresh mix if the infestation persists. Neem soil drenches can help for heavy cases.
