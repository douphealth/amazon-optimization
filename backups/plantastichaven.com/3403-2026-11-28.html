Quick answer: Philodendron 'Splendid' is a hybrid of Philodendron melanochrysum and Philodendron verrucosum, grown for large velvety dark-green leaves with pale veins. It wants bright indirect light, a chunky airy aroid mix kept lightly moist but never soggy, humidity above about 60%, temperatures of 18-27 C, and a moss pole to climb. Most problems on this plant trace back to soggy dense soil or dry air, not to under-feeding.

What Philodendron 'Splendid' actually is

'Splendid' is a cross between Philodendron melanochrysum and Philodendron verrucosum. It inherits the elongated leaf shape and velvet texture of melanochrysum and the sturdier, broader form and petiole hairs of verrucosum. In practice that makes it noticeably more forgiving than verrucosum on its own, which is why it is often recommended as a first velvet-leaved philodendron. It is a climbing epiphyte: given something to attach to, aerial roots grip the support and each new leaf comes in larger than the last. Left to trail without support, leaf size stalls and then regresses.

Light: the variable that sets leaf size

[ph_cer claim="Velvet-leaved philodendrons need bright indirect light, not low light." evidence="New leaves emerge smaller than the previous ones, stems stretch and the gap between leaves lengthens." reasoning="Velvety leaf surfaces scatter incoming light rather than reflecting it, so the plant needs more ambient light than a glossy-leaved philodendron to fix the same amount of carbon. Direct midday sun, however, scorches the matte surface because it cannot shed heat the way a waxy leaf can." source="RHS houseplant guidance" source_url="https://www.rhs.org.uk/houseplants"]

A position a metre or so back from an east or north window, or behind a sheer curtain at a west or south window, suits it well. If you are growing under a lamp, aim the light so the leaf surface is evenly lit rather than hot-spotted directly beneath the diode.

Watering and soil

[ph_cer claim="Root rot on 'Splendid' is a soil-structure problem before it is a watering-frequency problem." evidence="Lower leaves yellow and drop while the mix stays visibly damp for many days after watering; stems feel soft at the base." reasoning="These are epiphytic climbers whose roots evolved on bark and leaf litter, where water drains away in minutes and air reaches the root surface constantly. In a dense peat-based mix the water sits, the air spaces stay flooded, and the roots suffocate and then rot. Watering less often in the same dense mix slows the damage but does not remove the cause." source="University of Minnesota Extension, houseplant care" source_url="https://extension.umn.edu/find-plants/houseplants"]

Use a mix you can see through: orchid bark, perlite and coco coir with a little sphagnum, in a pot with a real drainage hole. Water thoroughly until it runs out of the base, then let the top few centimetres dry before watering again. In that kind of mix it is genuinely hard to overwater.

Philodendron Splendid vs. Philodendron Verrucosum

These velvet-leaved climbers overlap in their basic needs, but they are not interchangeable. Both benefit from bright indirect light, an airy aroid mix, and a stable climbing support. Verrucosum is generally less forgiving of dry air and roots that remain wet for too long, while Splendid should still be protected from abrupt humidity drops and dense, waterlogged soil.

Humidity and leaf damage

[ph_cer claim="Crispy brown leaf edges on 'Splendid' indicate dry air or inconsistent watering, not a nutrient deficiency." evidence="Browning starts at the leaf margin and tip while the rest of the leaf keeps normal colour and the veins stay pale and clear." reasoning="Margins and tips are the last places the plant's vascular system delivers water to. When air is dry, transpiration outpaces uptake and those cells at the end of the line dry out first. A nutrient problem instead shows as a colour change across the whole leaf blade or between the veins, because it affects chlorophyll production rather than water supply." source="PlantasticHaven diagnostic framework"]

Above roughly 60% relative humidity, leaves come in wider and hold their velvet. Below about 40%, expect smaller leaves and crisping edges. A humidifier near the plant works; misting does not raise ambient humidity for long enough to matter.

Give it something to climb

This is the step people skip. Aerial roots need to grip a damp, textured surface — a moss pole, a coir pole or a slab of tree fern. Once they attach, the plant reads the support as a trunk and starts producing its mature, larger leaves. Keep the pole damp, and pin new growth against it rather than tying it tightly.

Common problems at a glance

  • Yellow lower leaves, damp soil: soggy or dense mix. Repot into a chunkier aroid mix and check the pot drains.
  • Crispy edges, healthy colour: dry air. Raise humidity.
  • Small new leaves, long bare stem: not enough light, or no support to climb.
  • Silvery stippling on leaves, fine webbing: spider mites. Isolate and treat.
  • Deformed, scarred new growth: thrips. Isolate and treat repeatedly; they hide in unfurling leaves.

Pet safety: like all philodendrons, 'Splendid' contains calcium oxalate crystals and is toxic to cats and dogs if chewed. Keep it out of reach of pets that sample plants.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much light does a philodendron need?

Philodendrons prefer bright, indirect light. They can tolerate low light but will grow slower and produce smaller leaves.

Why are my philodendron's leaves turning yellow?

Yellow leaves are usually caused by overwatering, too much direct sun, or nutrient deficiency. Check soil moisture and light conditions first.

How do I propagate a philodendron?

Take stem cuttings with at least one node and leaf. Root in water for 2-4 weeks or directly in moist soil. Philodendrons propagate easily.

Is philodendron toxic to pets?

Yes — philodendrons contain calcium oxalate crystals that can cause mouth irritation, drooling, and vomiting in cats and dogs if ingested.

How often should I water my philodendron?

Water when the top 2 inches of soil are dry. Most philodendrons need watering every 1-2 weeks, depending on humidity and light levels.
