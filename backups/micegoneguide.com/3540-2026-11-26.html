Best bait for mouse traps

UC IPM identifies peanut butter as a popular bait because it is easy to use and attractive to house mice; it also notes allergy risks.[2] But the best bait is not always the same in every home. If mice are chewing cereal, oats, bird seed, pet food, chocolate, crackers, or nuts, use that clue. The bait should match what they are already investigating.

UC IPM identifies cotton wool or dental floss as possible nesting-material baits, particularly for reproductive females. Use a small amount on the trigger plate and follow the trap instructions.[2] The key is to use very little. A trap overloaded with bait becomes a free feeding station.

How much bait to use

Use a pea-sized amount or less. The bait should force the mouse to work at the trigger. If the mouse can lick or remove the bait from the side without stepping on the trigger, the trap is poorly baited. Smear sticky bait into the trigger cup or use a toothpick to apply a tiny amount. For snap traps, set the trigger facing the wall when placed perpendicular to the wall, or use pairs where mice may jump obstacles.

How to orient baited snap traps (T-placement)

Set each mouse-sized snap trap flush against the wall with the baited trigger end against the baseboard so the trap forms a T with the wall. Check traps daily and keep all traps and bait out of reach of children and pets—no trap is automatically pet-safe. For full room-by-room placement maps, see our dedicated guide on where to place mouse traps.[1]

Why bait fails

Start with route, competing food, bait quantity and trigger setup—not an unsourced cheese-versus-sweets ranking. CDC’s pre-bait advice chiefly addresses cautious rats and says house mice are less cautious; this is not evidence for a mandatory 48-hour mouse pre-baiting routine.[1] UC IPM advises a small amount of bait and notes that nesting materials may be useful for reproductive females.[2]

  • Too much bait lets mice steal it.
  • Wrong location misses the active route.
  • Use enough suitably placed traps to address observed activity; no universal count or result date is established here.[2]
  • Food is still available elsewhere, so mice ignore the trap.
  • Children, pets, or household traffic force traps into weak locations.
  • Droppings were cleaned away before the active route was mapped.

Why CDC advises against live traps and glue traps

The CDC advises against using live traps or glue traps because caught rodents become stressed and may urinate, increasing household exposure to rodent-borne diseases. Use mouse-sized snap traps baited with chunky peanut butter instead.

Trap bait around children and pets

Do not place open snap traps where a child, dog, cat, or non-target animal can contact them. Use protected locations behind appliances only when truly inaccessible, or choose enclosed devices designed to reduce access risk. Never use rodenticide bait casually. If poison bait is used, follow the label exactly and use tamper-resistant bait stations as required. For many homes, trapping is preferred because it avoids dead mouse odor inside walls and does not rely on toxic bait.

What to do after a catch

Wear gloves. Treat dead rodents and traps as contaminated. Disinfect before handling reusable traps, bag waste safely, and wash hands. Then reset traps until activity stops. One catch does not prove the infestation is over. Continue food control, entry-point sealing, and monitoring for new droppings.[4]

Final checklist before you act

  • Keep children, pets, and unnecessary people away from contaminated or trapped areas.
  • Photograph droppings, gnaw marks, nests, rub marks, food damage, or possible entry holes before cleaning or sealing.
  • Remove food access first: pet food, bird seed, pantry spills, trash, snacks, and cardboard clutter.
  • Place traps only on confirmed active routes and only where they can be used safely.
  • Wet droppings and nesting material with disinfectant before wiping or disposal.[4]
  • Seal gaps with durable, chew-resistant materials after active routes are controlled.
  • Continue checking the same locations for new evidence; a fixed number of quiet days does not guarantee that the home is clear.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my mouse trap not catching anything even with bait?

Check placement, bait quantity, trigger setup and competing food first. CDC discusses pre-baiting mainly for cautious rats and says house mice are less cautious; it does not establish a universal two-day pre-bait rule for mice.[1][2]

What is the best bait for mouse traps?

CDC describes chunky peanut butter or mutton fat as snap-trap baits. UC IPM says the best bait is often the food house mice are already eating and notes peanut allergy risks. Use a small amount that suits the trap instructions, not a universal ranked bait list.[1][2]

How much bait should I use on a mouse trap?

CDC’s prevention brochure describes a pea-sized amount on a snap-trap bait pan. UC IPM advises using a small amount on the trigger plate because too much may be removed without triggering the trap. Follow the model instructions.[5][2]

Where should I put baited mouse traps?

Place traps along walls, behind appliances, in corners, behind objects, and near droppings or gnaw marks. Do not place traps randomly in the open center of a room.

Can mice learn to avoid traps?

A missed catch does not by itself establish learned avoidance. Check the active route, bait quantity and trigger setup. CDC says house mice are less cautious than rats; this page does not prescribe a generic pre-baiting period.[1][2]

Is bait safe around pets and children?

Keep every trap and bait inaccessible to children, pets and non-target animals. An enclosed mechanism does not establish that access is safe. Check food allergy constraints as well as the trap instructions.[1][2]
