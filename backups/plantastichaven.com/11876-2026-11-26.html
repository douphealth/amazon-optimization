If you share your home with a cat, you have probably caught them mid-crime: nose buried in your spider plant, a leaf dangling from their mouth like a green mustache. It is one of the most common sights in cat-owning plant households — and it raises the obvious question: are spider plants toxic to cats?

The short answer

No — spider plants (Chlorophytum comosum) are not toxic to cats. The ASPCA officially lists the spider plant as non-toxic to cats and dogs. Your cat may still vomit if they eat a lot of it, but that is mechanical irritation from the fibrous leaves, not poisoning. The real danger is mistaking a toxic lookalike — like dracaena — for a spider plant.

Key takeaways

  • Spider plants are officially non-toxic to cats and dogs according to the ASPCA — this is not a gray area.
  • Why the obsession? Dangling leaves trigger your cat's play instinct, and the crisp texture mimics the grass cats naturally chew for hairball relief.
  • The "it gets cats high" story is internet folklore. No peer-reviewed study supports it; veterinarians explicitly say the evidence isn't there.
  • Big meals can still cause vomiting or diarrhea — from fiber overload and possible pesticide residue, not from plant toxins.
  • The genuine risk is misidentification: dracaena (also called "ribbon plant"), pothos, lilies, and sago palm look similar enough to confuse — and they are toxic.
  • Best protection: hang the plant out of reach and give your cat their own cat grass to chew instead.

Why cats are obsessed with spider plants

Spider plants might as well have been designed in a feline focus group. The long, arching leaves dangle and sway with every draft, triggering the same stalk-and-pounce instinct your cat uses on toys — and on your ankles at 3 a.m. The baby plantlets ("spiderettes") hanging from the mother plant are the perfect size for batting. And the leaves have a satisfying crispness when chewed that many cats find irresistible.

There is also a plausible biological reason. Cats in the wild chew grass, and veterinarians note that plant fiber can act as a laxative or emetic, which may help cats pass hairballs. A spider plant's strappy leaves are, to a cat, basically indoor grass with better PR.

The "spider plants get cats high" claim, honestly examined

You will find dozens of websites claiming spider plants contain compounds that give cats a mild, catnip-like euphoria. Here is the truth: there is no peer-reviewed evidence for this at all. Dr. Wendy Wilkins, DVM, PhD, writing for Cat-Bytes, states plainly that "there isn't solid scientific evidence to support these claims," and cat behavior writer Julia Wilson notes there are no references to actual studies — her own informal test on four cats didn't back it up either. The "terpenoid" theory traces back to a TikTok video, not veterinary literature.

What is real science: a 2021 study in Science Advances showed cats damage catnip and silver vine leaves to release mosquito-repelling compounds and then rub them into their fur. Fascinating — but it studied catnip and silver vine only, and says nothing about spider plants. So file the spider-plant high under internet folklore: harmless to believe, dishonest to present as fact.

What happens if your cat eats too much spider plant

Because the plant itself is non-toxic, a nibble here and there is a non-event. Problems start with volume. Eating a large quantity of any fibrous plant material can cause:

  • Vomiting — the most common outcome, usually within a few hours
  • Diarrhea — from the sheer amount of indigestible fiber
  • Drooling or reduced appetite — mild oral irritation from the rough leaf edges

These symptoms are typically mild and resolve on their own. But there are two hidden risks that have nothing to do with the plant's own chemistry:

  • Pesticide and fertilizer residue. Commercially grown spider plants are often treated with chemicals, and additives like bone meal in potting soil can be harmful to cats. If your cat is a dedicated chewer, buy organic plants or rinse a new plant thoroughly and repot it in plain, pet-safe potting mix.
  • Large amounts of any plant material can, in rare cases, contribute to a gastrointestinal blockage — this is true of "safe" plants generally, per PetMD.

🚨 When to call the vet

Call your vet or a poison hotline if vomiting or diarrhea persists or worsens, if your cat becomes lethargic or stops eating, or — critically — if you are not 100% sure the plant was a spider plant. A misidentified dracaena or lily is a genuine emergency.

The real danger: toxic lookalikes

Here is the twist most articles bury: the spider plant itself is the safe one — the danger is its evil twins. Several genuinely toxic houseplants share its strappy leaves or its nicknames, and mix-ups happen constantly. The ASPCA itself lists "Ribbon Plant" as a common name for both the spider plant (Chlorophytum comosum) AND for dracaena species — the name collision is documented at the source. If you bought a plant labeled "ribbon plant," verify the botanical name before assuming it's safe.

5 myths about spider plants and cats — debunked

Myth #1

"Spider plants are poisonous to cats."
False. The ASPCA's official listing is unambiguous: non-toxic to cats and dogs. This myth survives because of the lookalike problem above — people blame the spider plant for what a dracaena or pothos did.

Myth #2

"Spider plants get cats high."
Unproven internet folklore. No peer-reviewed study supports a euphoric effect, and veterinarians explicitly say the evidence isn't there. Cats act goofy around spider plants because they're fun to attack, not because they're pharmacological.

Myth #3

"Non-toxic means my cat can eat as much as it wants."
False. "Safe for cats" is not the same as "safe to eat." PetMD notes that any large quantity of plant material can cause vomiting, diarrhea, or even a GI blockage. Non-toxic is a chemistry verdict, not a dietary recommendation.

Myth #4

"All strappy-leaf hanging plants are safe."
Dangerously false. Dracaena, spider lily, and daylilies can all be confused with spider plants by appearance or name — and all are toxic to cats. Always verify the botanical name, Chlorophytum comosum.

Myth #5

"My cat vomited after chewing it, so it must be poisonous."
Usually not. Vomiting after a leaf binge is typically mechanical irritation from fibrous leaves eaten too fast — the feline equivalent of eating too much salad too quickly. True poisoning looks different: persistent vomiting, lethargy, tremors, or collapse.

Spider plant care basics for cat households

A healthy spider plant survives cat attention far better than a stressed one. The care is famously easy:

  • Light: bright, indirect light is ideal; it tolerates lower light but direct sun scorches the leaf tips.
  • Water: water when the top 1–2 inches of soil are dry. Spider plants are sensitive to fluoride and chlorine in tap water — brown leaf tips are the classic symptom. Distilled or rainwater fixes it.
  • Soil & fertilizer: standard well-draining potting mix. In a cat household, skip chemical-heavy fertilizers and bone-meal additives — plain organic mix is the safest choice for a plant your cat will inevitably taste-test.
  • Propagation bonus: those dangling babies root in water in days. Free plants for every room — and cheap replacements for the one your cat eventually defeats.

Frequently asked questions

Are spider plants toxic to cats?
No. The ASPCA officially lists Chlorophytum comosum (spider plant) as non-toxic to cats and dogs. Eating large amounts may cause mild vomiting or diarrhea from fiber overload, but there is no toxic compound involved.
Are spider plants toxic to dogs?
No — the ASPCA's listing covers dogs too: "Non-Toxic to Dogs, Non-Toxic to Cats." The same caveats apply: a dog that eats half the plant may still vomit from the sheer volume of fiber.
Why does my cat keep eating my spider plant?
Three reasons: the dangling leaves trigger your cat's hunting instinct, the crisp texture is satisfying to chew, and the grass-like leaves mimic the wild grasses cats eat to help pass hairballs. Growing dedicated cat grass usually reduces the behavior.
Do spider plants get cats high?
There is no scientific evidence for this. The claim circulates widely online but traces to social media rather than veterinary research, and veterinarians explicitly say the evidence isn't there. Cats act playful around spider plants because they're fun, not because they're psychoactive.
My cat ate spider plant leaves and vomited. Should I worry?
A single vomiting episode after a leaf binge is usually just mechanical irritation and resolves on its own. Monitor your cat for a few hours. Call your vet if vomiting repeats, diarrhea persists, your cat becomes lethargic or stops eating — or if you're not certain the plant was actually a spider plant.
Are spider plant babies (spiderettes) safe for cats?
Yes. The baby plantlets are the same plant — Chlorophytum comosum — and equally non-toxic. They are, however, the part cats find most irresistible, so they're usually the first casualty.
What plants look like spider plants but are toxic to cats?
Dracaena (also sold as "ribbon plant" — a documented name collision), pothos, peace lily, lilies, and sago palm. Dracaena is the most common mix-up: it has stiffer leaves and a woody trunk, while true spider plants have soft arching leaves and no trunk. Always check the botanical name.
How do I keep my cat away from my spider plant?
Hang the plant out of reach (the most effective method), grow cat grass as an approved alternative, try bitter apple spray on the leaves, cover the soil with pebbles or foil, and use citrus peels or double-sided tape as deterrents. Avoid cayenne pepper indoors — it can irritate your cat's eyes and nose.
Is spider plant soil dangerous for cats?
The plant is safe, but potting soil can carry risks: chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and additives like bone meal can harm cats. Use plain organic potting mix for plants your cat chews, and rinse new nursery plants before bringing them home.
Are spider plants safe for kittens?
Yes, spider plants are non-toxic to kittens as well as adult cats. Kittens are even more enthusiastic chewers, though, so hang the plant well out of reach and provide kitten-safe chew alternatives like cat grass.
