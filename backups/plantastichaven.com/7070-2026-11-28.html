Quick answer: Pothos (Epipremnum aureum) is a low-maintenance houseplant. NC State Extension says it prefers bright, indirect light, survives for long periods in low light, and should have its well-drained potting mix dry out between waterings. It is poisonous to dogs, cats and horses according to the ASPCA, so keep it out of reach of pets.

Pothos varieties: pick the look you want

All pothos sold as houseplants are cultivars of Epipremnum aureum — the species name is Latin for "golden," according to NC State Extension. Care is essentially the same across varieties, but light needs differ: NC State notes that low light can cause loss of variegation, so the whiter and more variegated the leaves, the brighter the spot it needs to hold its pattern. Solid-green types are the most forgiving of dim rooms — NC State specifically calls 'Jade' "great in low light."

If you are choosing between two, match the variety to the light you actually have rather than the light you wish you had.

Light: aim for bright and indirect, expect it to cope with less

NC State Extension describes pothos as preferring bright, indirect light while surviving for long periods in low light, and UF/IFAS lists golden pothos among low-light houseplants. The trade-off is colour: NC State notes that low light can cause loss of variegation, so a golden, Marble Queen or Snow Queen pothos kept in a dim spot may turn greener. Its cultivar notes describe ‘Jade’ as solid dark green and “great in low light”. To compare rooms and windows, see our guides to plant light requirements and houseplants for low light.

Watering: check the pot, not the calendar

University of Maryland Extension advises that plants should not be watered on a schedule. Test the soil with a finger to a depth of about two inches, or lift the pot and notice how much lighter it is when dry. It says a plant that is too dry wilts and turns gray-green, while one that is too wet drops leaves or turns yellow. For pothos specifically, NC State warns that overwatering can cause root rot and blackening of the leaf margins.

When it is time to water, Maryland Extension says to water until the water drains freely from the bottom, dump any excess from the saucer, never let the pot sit in water, and wait until the mix has dried at the two-inch depth before watering again. Our pothos watering guide goes through the checks step by step.

Pothos slows down in winter — NC State's feeding guidance treats winter as the dormant period — so the mix stays wet longer and watering stretches out. In active growth (spring and summer) check the pot more often; in winter, check less and be slower to reach for the can. The finger test from Maryland Extension works year-round: two inches dry means water, damp means wait.

Soil, pot and repotting

University of Maryland Extension says a potting mix should be porous enough for root aeration and drainage while still holding water and nutrients. It warns that products sold as “potting soil” are usually too dense unless loosened with perlite or vermiculite, and suggests a soil-less mix instead. Use a pot with a drainage hole.

Maryland Extension also notes that if you cannot push a finger two inches into the mix, you may need a more porous mix or the plant may be root-bound. When you repot, its steps are to loosen circling roots, cut away any dead brown roots, set the root ball an inch or two below the rim, water thoroughly, and then avoid watering again until the top of the mix dries. See our repotting guide for the full routine.

Temperature and humidity

UF/IFAS says houseplants do fine at 65 to 75°F and that a long period below 50°F may cause permanent damage. University of Maryland Extension gives 70 to 80°F by day and 60 to 68°F at night as the range where foliage houseplants generally grow best. Both advise keeping plants away from vents and other hot or cold spots. For humidity, NC State suggests a humidifier or a pebble tray, and Maryland Extension adds that grouping plants raises humidity in their area while misting is of questionable value.

Feeding

NC State suggests fertilizing pothos every other month except when the plant is dormant in winter. UF/IFAS says most indoor plants grow fairly slowly and do not need much fertilizer; follow the product label.

In practice, "every other month" (NC State) means roughly three to four feedings across the growing season for most homes. UF/IFAS stresses following the product label — a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer mixed to label strength is the straightforward choice. Signs you are overdoing it mirror the salt build-up row in the problems table: crust on the pot, brown leaf tips, and stalled growth. If you see those, flush the pot with clear water until it drains freely and skip the next feeding; Maryland Extension suggests leaching with clear water every four to six months as routine maintenance.

Pruning, support and fuller growth

According to NC State, older leaves yellow and fall off over time, which leaves most of the foliage at the ends of the stems. Pruning stems back helps keep the plant bushy. Pothos climbs by brown aerial roots, and with a support to climb and enough light it begins to produce larger, mature leaves. NC State also suggests wiping the leaves occasionally with a soft cloth to remove dust.

To prune for fullness, cut a stem just above a leaf node — the bump where a leaf meets the stem. NC State's advice to prune stems back to maintain a bushy habit works because each cut typically prompts branching below it. Do the main cutback as growth resumes in spring, and trim stray vines any time they outgrow their spot. Every healthy cutting with a node is a free plant; see the propagation section below rather than binning them. Wipe blades between plants if you have had pest issues.

Training pothos to climb

Pothos climbs by brown aerial roots, and NC State notes that with a support to climb and enough light it begins to produce larger, mature leaves — dramatically bigger than the juvenile foliage on a trailing plant. A moss or coir pole gives the aerial roots something to grip; tie the longest stems loosely to the pole and tuck aerial roots against it when you water. Expect slow progress at first — the plant invests in roots before leaves — and keep the light bright and indirect, since a climbing pothos in a dim corner will reach without ever sizing up its leaves. Our care basics above still apply: the climbing plant wants the same water, soil, and feeding, just with vertical ambition.

Propagation

NC State Extension notes that pothos vines root easily in water, which makes this one of the simplest houseplants to multiply. Our pothos propagation guide covers the method in detail; the essentials are below.

Water method: cut a healthy stem just below a node (look for the small brown aerial-root bump), strip the lowest leaf, and stand the cutting in a jar of room-temperature water with the node submerged. Change the water weekly. Roots usually appear within a couple of weeks; move the cutting to potting mix once roots are a few inches long, and keep the mix evenly moist for the first week while it adjusts.

Soil method: press the node directly into moist potting mix, node buried and leaf above the surface. Keep the mix lightly moist — not wet — until new growth shows, which signals roots have formed. Soil-rooted cuttings skip transplant shock, so this suits anyone who tends to forget water changes.

Take cuttings during active growth for fastest rooting, and always start with more cuttings than you need; not every node wakes up. Highly variegated varieties sometimes revert to greener growth from cuttings — if the pattern matters to you, propagate from the most variegated stems.

Choosing a healthy pothos

A good nursery plant has firm stems, leaves that are flat and evenly coloured for their variety, and soil that is lightly moist rather than soggy or bone-dry. Check the undersides of a few leaves and the leaf joints for the mealybugs and scale that NC State says to monitor for — white cottony clusters or small bumps are a pass. Avoid plants with widespread yellowing (beyond the odd older leaf, which NC State says is normal) or blackened leaf margins, which point to the overwatering damage NC State describes. A slightly root-bound plant is fine; a plant with roots circling densely out of the drainage hole wants repotting on arrival.

Pothos and pets

The ASPCA lists pothos (devil’s ivy) as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. The toxic principle is insoluble calcium oxalates, and the listed signs are oral irritation, pain and swelling of the mouth, tongue and lips, excessive drooling, vomiting (not horses) and difficulty swallowing. If you suspect a pet has eaten it, contact your veterinarian or the ASPCA Poison Control line, (888) 426-4435; the ASPCA notes a consultation fee may apply. More detail is in our pothos pet-safety guide.

Is it a pothos or a philodendron?

NC State says pothos is distinguished from heartleaf philodendron by the absence of conspicuous free stipules on new growth and by grooved petioles. Our pothos vs philodendron guide shows what to look for.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I water a pothos?

There is no fixed interval. Water when the mix has dried about two inches down or the pot feels light, then water until it drains and empty the saucer.

Can a pothos live in low light?

NC State says it survives for long periods in low light, but low light can cause variegated types to lose their variegation, and it prefers bright, indirect light.

Is pothos safe for cats and dogs?

No. The ASPCA lists it as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. Keep it where pets cannot reach it.

Why are my pothos leaves turning yellow?

Overwatering is a common cause, and older leaves also yellow and drop naturally. Check the pot’s moisture and drainage first; the table above and our yellow-leaves guide cover the rest.

How fast does pothos grow?

Outdoors, vines can reach 40 feet (NC State); indoors, growth is far more modest and depends on light. A pothos in bright, indirect light with regular feeding through the growing season can add a foot or more of vine a year, while one in low light creeps along. If yours has stalled, light is the first thing to check.

Can pothos live in water permanently?

Cuttings root easily in water (NC State) and can live in a jar for months, but water alone has no nutrients, so long-term water culture leads to pale, slow growth unless you add a dilute hydroponic fertilizer. For a full, vigorous plant, potting mix remains the better home.

Why is my pothos leggy with bare stems?

Long gaps between leaves usually mean the plant is reaching for more light. Move it toward brighter, indirect light and prune the bare stems back — NC State's guidance is that pruning maintains a bushy habit, and the cuttings root readily (see propagation above).

Should I mist my pothos?

Maryland Extension says it is questionable whether misting meaningfully raises humidity. A humidifier or pebble tray (NC State) does more, and pothos tolerates average household humidity anyway — brown, crispy leaf edges in a dry winter room are the main sign it wants more moisture in the air.

When should I repot my pothos?

When you cannot push a finger two inches into the mix, or roots circle the drainage hole — both cues come from Maryland Extension. Most pothos need it every one to two years; going up just one pot size avoids a swamp of unused wet mix.

Is pothos the same as devil's ivy?

Yes — devil's ivy is the common name the ASPCA uses for pothos (Epipremnum aureum). It is the same plant, and the same toxicity warnings apply.
