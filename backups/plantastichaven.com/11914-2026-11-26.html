Quick answer: Is string of pearls hard to care for?

No — string of pearls (Curio rowleyanus) is easy once you master watering. Give it bright indirect light, a gritty fast-draining mix, and water only when the soil is fully dry. Overwatering kills more string of pearls than everything else combined. Keep it away from cats and dogs: it is toxic if eaten.

What is string of pearls?

String of pearls is a trailing succulent whose bead-like leaves cascade over pot edges like a green necklace. Botanically it is Curio rowleyanus (H.Jacobsen) P.V.Heath, a member of the daisy family (Asteraceae). If you have seen it sold as Senecio rowleyanus, that is the same plant — the older name is still widespread in nurseries and garden centers, but Curio rowleyanus is the currently accepted name.

The species is native to the dry regions of South Africa's Cape Provinces (and southern Namibia), where it creeps along the ground, rooting wherever its stems touch the soil and forming dense mats. In the wild it typically grows tucked under shrubs and rocks, avoiding harsh direct sun — a habit that explains its preference for bright but filtered light indoors. The specific epithet rowleyanus honors Gordon Douglas Rowley (1921–2019), the British botanist who specialized in cacti and succulents and first described the species in 1968.

Each "pearl" is a modified leaf about 6–8 mm across — nearly spherical, with a tiny pointed tip and a thin translucent stripe called a window. That window lets light penetrate into the leaf's interior, so the plant photosynthesizes from inside as well as outside the leaf. The spherical shape is itself an adaptation: it minimizes the surface area exposed to dry desert air, reducing water loss. Stems trail 60–90 cm (2–3 feet) and root readily at the nodes.

In summer, mature plants produce clusters of small white, brush-like flowers on stalks a few centimeters long. The blooms are often described as smelling faintly of cinnamon or cloves and last roughly a month. Flowering is most likely when the plant gets a cool, dry rest in winter — more on that below.

Two forms are commonly sold:

  • Standard green — solid blue-green pearls; the hardiest and fastest-growing form.
  • Variegated (Curio rowleyanus 'Variegatus') — pearls streaked with cream, sometimes blushing pink in bright light. A cultivated sport, slower-growing and fussier: it needs more light to keep its variegation and is more prone to sunburn and rot. Expect to pay more and baby it a little.

Light requirements

Light is the variable most new owners get wrong, usually by giving too little. String of pearls wants bright, indirect light for most of the day — think a spot a meter or so back from a sunny window, or right in an east-facing window that gets gentle morning sun. Outdoors (USDA zones 9b–11b only) it prefers partial shade, mimicking the shrub-and-rock shelter of its native habitat.

The top of the pot matters more than the trailing strands. The crown — where stems emerge from the soil — must receive good light, or the plant thins out at the base while the strands keep growing. A hanging basket jammed into a dark corner with only the strands in light will go bald on top. When in doubt, lower the hanger or raise the plant so light falls directly onto the soil surface.

Not sure whether your spot counts as bright indirect, medium, or low light? Our plant light requirements guide breaks down every light level with window-direction examples and a simple hand-shadow test you can do in seconds.

Signs your light is off:

  • Too little light: widely spaced pearls on stretched, thin stems (etiolation); new growth smaller and paler; variegated forms reverting toward plain green.
  • Too much direct sun: pearls turning yellowish, bleached, or developing scorched brown patches — most common on a hot south-facing sill in midsummer.

A practical test: hold your hand about 30 cm above the plant at midday. A soft, defined shadow means good bright-indirect light. A razor-sharp dark shadow means harsh direct sun (pull it back or add a sheer curtain). Barely any shadow means too dim — move it closer to the window.

Watering mastery (the #1 killer)

More string of pearls die from overwatering than from every other cause combined. The pearls are water tanks: the plant is built to endure drought, not constant moisture. The NC State Extension's guidance is blunt — it tolerates infrequent watering, but overwatering leads to root rot. The method that works is soak and dry: water thoroughly until it drains from the bottom, then wait until the soil is completely dry before watering again.

In practice that means watering far less often than you think — roughly every 2–3 weeks in the growing season for a typical indoor pot, and as little as once a month in winter, when cooler temperatures and lower light slow growth to a crawl. Always adjust to your conditions rather than a calendar: a small terracotta pot in a warm bright room dries much faster than a large plastic pot in a cool corner.

The principle — water based on soil dryness, not the calendar — applies to almost every houseplant you own. Our dryness-first watering guide teaches you to read the soil like a pro instead of guessing.

How to tell when it's time:

  • The squeeze test: gently squeeze a pearl. A well-hydrated pearl feels firm like a plump pea. A thirsty pearl feels slightly soft and gives a little — that is your cue to water within a day or two.
  • The soil test: stick a finger or a wooden chopstick 3–5 cm into the mix. If it comes out with any damp soil clinging, wait. Water only when it is fully dry.
  • The weight test: lift the pot after watering and memorize the heavy feel. When it feels noticeably light, the soil has dried.

Watering technique matters too. Water at soil level or bottom-water (set the pot in a dish of water for 15–20 minutes so moisture wicks up) rather than showering the strands — wet pearls sitting against soggy soil invite rot at the crown. Always empty the saucer; never let the pot sit in standing water.

Winter rule: from late autumn through winter, cut watering roughly in half and keep the plant cooler (around 50–60 °F / 10–16 °C if you can). This cool, dry rest is also what triggers summer flowering.

Soil & potting

String of pearls needs a mix that drains fast and dries out between waterings. Standard houseplant potting soil holds far too much moisture on its own. Use a commercial cactus/succulent mix, or build your own gritty blend:

For exact ingredient ratios and three proven DIY recipes, see our succulent soil mix guide for indoor pots — it covers drainage science and which ingredients to avoid.

Ingredient Ratio Why it helps
Cactus/succulent potting mix 2 parts Base nutrition and structure
Perlite or pumice 1 part Aeration; keeps mix open so roots breathe
Coarse sand or fine gravel 1 part Drainage; mimics gritty native soils

Pot choice matters as much as the mix. Terracotta is ideal — it wicks moisture through its walls so the soil dries faster and more evenly. Whatever you choose, it must have drainage holes. Shallow, wide pots suit the plant's shallow root system better than deep narrow ones, and they dry out faster. When the plant is young, keep the pot snug: too much empty soil stays wet too long and invites rot.

Top-dressing the soil surface with a thin layer of coarse grit or small pebbles is worth doing. It keeps the lowest pearls off damp soil, which is where crown rot usually starts.

Temperature & humidity

String of pearls likes the same temperatures you do in summer — around 70–80 °F (21–27 °C) — and prefers a distinct cool-down in winter to roughly 50–60 °F (10–16 °C). That winter chill, combined with reduced watering, is what sets up summer flowering. It is frost-intolerant: NC State Extension advises bringing plants indoors before the first fall frost, and even a brief dip near freezing can damage the water-filled pearls.

Keep it away from heat sources (radiators, heating vents) in winter and from cold drafts (leaky windows, exterior doors). Sudden temperature swings can cause pearl drop.

Humidity is refreshingly simple: average household humidity is fine, and drier is better than humid. Do not mist the plant — wet pearls in still air are a rot risk, and as a succulent it gains nothing from misting. Good air circulation matters more than humidity: a gently ventilated room prevents the stagnant, damp microclimate where fungal problems start.

Fertilizing

String of pearls evolved in nutrient-poor soils and needs very little feeding. During the active growing season (spring through early autumn), a half-strength balanced liquid fertilizer once a month — or a cactus/succulent formula at label strength — is plenty. Some growers feed every other watering at quarter strength with equal success; the key is restraint.

Do not fertilize in late autumn or winter when growth slows, and never fertilize a stressed, newly repotted, or diseased plant. Signs of over-fertilizing include salt crust on the soil surface, brown leaf tips, and sudden pearl drop after feeding. If you suspect salt buildup, flush the pot thoroughly with plain water and skip the next feeding.

Pruning & shaping

Pruning keeps string of pearls full rather than straggly, and every cutting is a free new plant (see propagation). Trim in spring or early summer when the plant is actively growing.

  • For fullness: pinch or snip the longest strands back by a third. This encourages branching just behind the cut.
  • For a bald crown: coil a few healthy strands in circles on top of the soil and pin them down lightly — they will root at the nodes and fill the center. This "chop and coil" rescue works better than hoping the crown regrows on its own.
  • Remove dead material: cut away any dried, mushy, or blackened strands at the base with sterile scissors. Never pull — you will uproot healthy neighbors.

Always use clean, sharp scissors or pruners, and wash your hands afterward: the sap can irritate skin, and the plant is toxic if ingested.

Propagation step by step

String of pearls is one of the easiest succulents to propagate, and stem cuttings root reliably in 3–4 weeks. There are three methods; all start the same way.

The steps below are tailored to trailing succulents — for the universal principles behind every propagation method that work on any houseplant, see our complete plant propagation guide.

Step 1 — Take cuttings. With sterile scissors, snip healthy strands 10–15 cm (4–6 inches) long. Choose firm, plump strands with no mushy or shriveled sections.

Step 2 — Callus. Lay the cuttings in a dry, shaded spot for 24 hours (some growers wait up to 3 days). The cut ends form a dry callus that resists rot once planted.

Step 3 — Root using one of these methods:

  • On top of soil (easiest, recommended): fill a shallow pot with damp cactus mix, lay the cuttings on the surface with the nodes touching the soil, and pin strands down with bent wire or floral pins. Mist lightly every few days to keep the surface barely moist. Roots grow down from the nodes; new strands sprout upward.
  • Buried in soil: strip the lowest pearls off, poke the bare stem end 2–3 cm into the mix, and firm gently. Keep barely moist until rooted, then treat as a mature plant.
  • In water: strip lower pearls and submerge the bare nodes in room-temperature water, keeping pearls above the waterline. Change cloudy water promptly. Transplant to soil once roots reach about 2–3 cm — waiting longer increases rot and transplant shock risk.

Step 4 — Aftercare. Keep cuttings in bright indirect light (not direct sun). Once you see new growth or feel resistance when you tug gently, roots have formed — shift to the normal soak-and-dry routine. Do not fertilize until the new plant is clearly established.

Repotting

String of pearls has a shallow, delicate root system and resents disturbance, so repot only when truly necessary — roughly every 2–3 years, or when roots circle the drainage holes or the soil has broken down and stays soggy. Spring is the best time.

  • Choose a pot only slightly larger (2–3 cm wider) with drainage holes; terracotta is ideal.
  • Handle by the root ball, never by yanking the strands — they snap off easily.
  • Use fresh gritty mix (see the soil recipe above).
  • After repotting, wait 3–5 days before watering to let any damaged roots heal, then resume soak-and-dry.
  • A good moment to coil long strands back onto the fresh soil surface for a fuller crown.

Toxicity & pet safety

Keep away from pets and children. The ASPCA lists string of pearls (Senecio rowleyanus) as toxic to cats, dogs, and horses. NC State Extension likewise notes the plant is toxic to both humans and pets.

All parts of the plant are considered toxic if ingested. Reported symptoms in pets include vomiting, drooling, diarrhea, and gastrointestinal upset; skin contact with the sap can cause irritation in sensitive individuals. The dangling strands are especially tempting to cats — hanging the plant is display-smart but not pet-proof on its own, since cats climb. If a pet chews a significant amount, contact your veterinarian or the ASPCA Poison Control hotline at (888) 426-4435.

Practical precautions for pet households:

  • Hang high and out of launch range from furniture — not just "high."
  • Consider a closed terrarium-style display or a room pets cannot access.
  • Sweep up fallen pearls promptly; they roll surprisingly far.
  • Wash hands after pruning or repotting.

Frequently asked questions

Is string of pearls hard to keep alive?

It is easy once you respect its watering needs. Most failures come from overwatering or soil that stays wet. Bright indirect light, gritty fast-draining mix, and a strict soak-and-dry routine covers almost everything that matters.

Why are my pearls turning mushy and translucent?

That is classic overwatering — the water-storing leaves are bursting from excess moisture, often with root rot underneath. Stop watering, unpot to check roots, trim rot, and repot into dry gritty mix. Propagate healthy strands as insurance.

Why are my pearls shriveling?

Shriveled, wrinkled pearls usually mean the plant is thirsty — give it a thorough soak. If pearls do not plump up within two days, the roots may be damaged (often from earlier overwatering), so unpot and inspect them.

How much light does string of pearls need?

Bright indirect light for most of the day, with a little gentle morning sun. Make sure light falls on the top of the pot, not just the trailing strands, or the crown goes bald. Too little light causes stretched, sparse growth; harsh midday sun scorches the pearls.

Can I propagate a single pearl (leaf)?

Single pearls without stem tissue rarely root — you need a stem section with nodes. Take 10–15 cm strands, let them callus for a day, then lay them on damp cactus mix and pin the nodes down. Roots form in 3–4 weeks.

Is string of pearls toxic to cats?

Yes. The ASPCA lists it as toxic to cats, dogs, and horses; ingestion can cause vomiting, drooling, and gastrointestinal upset. Keep it out of pets' reach and call your vet or ASPCA Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 if a pet eats a significant amount.

Why isn't my string of pearls flowering?

Flowering needs maturity plus a cool, dry winter rest (around 50–60 °F with reduced watering). Plants kept warm and watered year-round often skip blooming. Even with ideal conditions, young plants may take a couple of years.

String of pearls vs. string of bananas — which is easier?

String of bananas (Curio radicans) is generally more forgiving: it tolerates more sun and heat and is slightly less rot-prone. If you have killed pearls before, bananas are a good confidence-builder with a similar look.

How do I make my string of pearls fuller on top?

Coil healthy strands in circles on the soil surface and pin them down so the nodes touch the mix — they root and fill the crown. Also make sure the top of the pot gets direct light; a dark crown stays bald.

Can string of pearls grow outdoors?

Only in USDA zones 9b–11b, in partial shade with excellent drainage, and it must come indoors before the first frost. Everywhere else, grow it as a houseplant — it can summer outdoors in a sheltered spot once nights stay above 50 °F.
