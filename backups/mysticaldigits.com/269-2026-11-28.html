Gematria assigns numerical values to Hebrew letters and, in some Jewish interpretive traditions, compares words by their totals. In standard values, חי (chai, “life”) is ח (chet) = 8 plus י (yod) = 10, totaling 18. The practice appears in particular textual and mystical settings; it is not universal across Jewish communities.

How the standard calculation works

In the standard sequence, the first nine Hebrew letters are valued 1–9, the next nine 10–90, and the final four 100–400. Add the value of each letter in the written word; the resulting total can be used in textual comparison in some interpretive contexts.[1]

Worked example: חי (chai)

Chai means “life.” Its two letters have standard values chet (ח) = 8 and yod (י) = 10, so 8 + 10 = 18. My Jewish Learning describes 18 as a special number in Jewish tradition and notes the custom of giving gifts or charitable contributions in multiples of 18.[2] This is a cultural association and custom, not a universal rule for every Jewish person or community.

Final forms and alternate convention

Five Hebrew letters have special forms at the ends of words. The Breslev guide describes ordinary final-letter values as generally equal to the corresponding standard forms: 20, 40, 50, 80, and 90.[3] My Jewish Learning lists an extended 500–900 sequence for final forms.[1] Because conventions vary, identify which one is being used rather than treating 500–900 as universal.

Tradition versus evidence

My Jewish Learning describes gematria as a numerical system developed by practitioners of Kabbalah and used to interpret biblical texts; it says the method appeared periodically in Talmud and Midrash, was not central to rabbinic literature, and is important in Kabbalah.[1] This varied history cautions against describing gematria as a belief shared uniformly by all Jews. A numerical match documents a calculation; by itself, it does not prove a historical claim or establish a universally accepted interpretation.

Gematria is rooted in Hebrew letters and Jewish textual interpretation, unlike modern Western Life Path numerology, which applies number reduction to birth-date components. They use different inputs and traditions; one should not be presented as the other.

Frequently asked questions

Does every Jewish community use gematria?

No. The source describes different degrees and contexts of use, not a universal practice.[1]

Are final letters always worth 500–900?

No. The extended sequence is an alternate convention; the ordinary values discussed in the Breslev guide are 20, 40, 50, 80, and 90.[3]

Is gematria the same as Life Path numerology?

No. Gematria adds values assigned to Hebrew letters; Life Path numerology uses a birth date.
