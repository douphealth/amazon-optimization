Quick answer

Most indoor philodendrons should be kept above 60°F, with 65-80°F the safer everyday range. A brief dip near 55°F may not kill a healthy plant, but cold wet soil, drafts, and windowsill chill can cause leaf damage quickly. Temperatures near 50°F or frost exposure are high-risk and require immediate protection.

Who this is for / not for

Who this is for

  • You are moving philodendrons indoors for winter or protecting them near cold windows.
  • You want exact temperature zones instead of vague advice like keep it warm.
  • You need a rescue plan after a draft, shipping delay, or patio cold snap.

Who this is not for

  • You are planning a permanent in-ground tropical landscape and need regional planting design rather than general hardiness guidance.
  • You are trying to overwinter a damaged plant without any indoor light or heat source.

Clear definition

Cold tolerance is the lowest temperature a philodendron can experience before stress or tissue damage becomes likely. For houseplants, the number is not only air temperature. Root-zone temperature, wet soil, draft duration, plant health, and recent repotting all change how much cold a philodendron can survive.

Philodendron cold tolerance chart

These are conservative household action ranges, not experimentally established injury thresholds for every cultivar. For reference, UF/IFAS guidance for interiorscape philodendron recommends 70 to 80°F; University of Maryland Extension gives 70 to 80°F by day and 60 to 68°F at night as the range where foliage houseplants grow best; and UF/IFAS says temperatures below 50°F are too cool for heartleaf philodendron. The colder and wetter the root ball is, the faster the plant declines.

Why is there no exact number for your cultivar?

Because an exact injury threshold comes from a controlled trial, and almost nobody has run one per cultivar. The graded chart above is household guidance, not laboratory data. Any page that quotes an exact per-cultivar injury temperature without naming the trial it came from is guessing. When in doubt, use the general chart and the warmest spot in your home.

The temperature, moisture, and duration framework

A healthy philodendron can handle a short cool night better than a wet, recently repotted plant sitting against icy glass. Judge the full situation, not just the thermostat number.

Temperature: protect below 60°F and never expose to frost.

Moisture: cold wet soil is more dangerous than cool dry soil because roots slow down and oxygen drops.

Duration: one cool hour is different from three cold nights in a row.

Recovery condition: bright indirect light and stable warmth help the plant repair after a chill.

Seasonal temperature targets: day vs night, growing season vs winter

One number is not enough. A philodendron wants different nights in July and January, and the gap between day and night matters as much as the numbers themselves.

The extension sources agree on the shape of this table even when their exact numbers differ: the University of Maryland Extension gives 70–80°F by day and 60–68°F at night for foliage houseplants; Clemson gives 75–85°F by day and 65–70°F at night as ideal. The overlap of the two is the 65–75°F band most homes can actually hold. Dry winter air makes cold worse: heating dries the air, and a plant that is already chilled loses moisture it cannot replace. Keep plants away from radiators and hot-air vents, and group plants together or run a humidifier if leaf edges crisp in winter.

Cold damage or something else? A quick differential

Cold damage has a tell: it follows a cold event, and it shows up fast, often overnight. If nothing cold happened in the last few days, look at water, light, and humidity first.

Two details help separate cold from everything else. First, cold damage respects the cold event: the leaves that were closest to the glass or the draft mark first, and the damage appears within hours to a few days. Second, UF/IFAS lists yellowing of lower leaves after several hours at 33–40°F as a cold effect — so even a plant that never saw frost can show cold damage. If the damage is spreading day by day with no cold event behind it, think disease, not weather, and read why philodendron leaves turn brown and why philodendron leaves turn yellow before you change anything else.

How do I tell cold damage from overwatering?

Ask what happened first. Cold damage follows a cold night, a draft, or a cold journey, and it shows on the most exposed leaves within days. Overwatering shows as yellow lower leaves with soil that stays wet for days, and it builds slowly over weeks. Cold plus wet soil is the worst combination of all, because chilled roots take up almost no water and the mix stays wet long enough to rot them.

What makes one philodendron tougher than another

Two plants of the same variety can come through the same cold night very differently. These are the four factors that move the number:

Age and size. A big, established plant with a thick stem and a full root system has more tissue to lose and more stored energy to regrow with. A small cutting or a young plant in a 4-inch pot has almost no buffer — treat it as more sensitive than a mature plant of the same variety, and keep it warmer and further from cold glass.

Pot size. Soil is insulation. A plant in a large pot cools far more slowly than the same plant in a small pot, because there is more soil mass holding daytime warmth. Small pots on cold surfaces are the first to chill through.

Recent repotting. A plant repotted in the last two to four weeks has damaged roots that take up water poorly. Cold on top of that is a double stress. Never repot right before a cold snap, and never repot a cold-damaged plant until it is warm and growing again.

Acclimation. A plant that has spent autumn cooling gradually tolerates a cold night better than one moved straight from a warm room to a cold porch. The change matters as much as the temperature: a sudden sharp drop does more damage than a slow slide to the same number. This is why the first cold night of autumn causes more damage than a colder night in January.

None of this changes the chart above — it explains why the chart is a range, not a promise. If your plant is young, recently repotted, or in a small pot, use the warm end of every band.

How to protect a philodendron from cold damage

  1. Check night temperatures, not only daytime highs. Philodendrons are most often damaged during overnight drops.
  2. Move plants away from cold glass, exterior doors, unheated sunrooms, and AC vents.
  3. Raise pots off tile, stone, or concrete floors with a stand, cork mat, or saucer riser.
  4. Water less often in winter because cool, low-light plants use moisture slowly.
  5. Keep leaves from touching windows. Glass can be much colder than room air.
  6. Pause fertilizer during cold stress; feeding does not fix low temperature or low light.
  7. After exposure, wait several days before pruning. Some chilled tissue darkens slowly.
  8. Resume normal care only when new growth is steady and the root ball dries on a predictable schedule.
Practical rule: Change one variable at a time. Light, water, soil, and temperature interact, so changing everything at once makes the real cause harder to identify.

Cold spots in your home: six places colder than the thermostat says

Your thermostat reads the air at chest height in one room. Your plant lives somewhere else. These are the spots that quietly run colder than the room around them:

  • The windowsill. Glass loses heat all night. A leaf touching the glass can be far colder than the air a foot away — pull plants back 1–3 feet in winter.
  • Next to an exterior door. Every opening lets in a blast of cold air. Plants near the front door get chilled dozens of times a day.
  • Unheated sunrooms and porches. They feel warm in the afternoon sun and drop fast after dark.
  • On tile, stone, or concrete floors. Cold floors chill the pot from below, and cold roots are the part of the plant that recovers slowest.
  • Above a radiator or beside a hot-air vent. Hot and dry at the same time: the air is warm but the humidity crashes, and the leaves crisp.
  • Near an air-conditioning vent. Cold air blowing directly on leaves in summer causes the same chilling injury as a winter draft.

The fix for most of these is free: move the plant. A thermometer at leaf level tells you the truth about a spot in one night — if you only buy one tool for winter, make it that one.

Chilling and freezing are different

Tropical foliage plants such as philodendrons can be injured by cold well above freezing. This is called chilling injury. Freezing injury is different: ice forms in the tissue and leaves turn water-soaked, then dark and collapsed. A plant that comes through one cool night has not shown that the temperature is a safe long-term minimum, so treat the lowest temperature measured at the plant, not the thermostat reading, as the number that matters.

Will a philodendron survive a freeze?

No — not reliably, and never count on it. A light frost, a few hours just below 32°F, may only scorch the leaves of a big established plant. A hard freeze, hours below freezing, especially with wind, kills leaves and stems, and usually the roots of anything in a pot. Pots freeze first: a pot has no ground heat to draw on, so a potted philodendron is always more exposed than the same plant in the ground.

The one partial exception is the tree philodendron. UF/IFAS notes that mature selloum specimens tolerate temperatures slightly below freezing for brief periods, and a mature plant that loses its leaves to a light frost can sometimes push new growth from the base once warm weather returns. That is a maybe, not a plan — and it does not apply to the trailing and variegated types most people grow indoors.

When a freeze is forecast, do this:

  • Bring pots indoors. Even an unheated garage that stays above freezing is far better than the open patio.
  • If the plant must stay out for one night, move it against a sheltered wall under cover and drape frost cloth over it. Cloth, not plastic — plastic transfers the cold straight to the leaves.
  • Water the soil earlier in the day, not the leaves. Damp soil holds daytime heat longer than dry soil.
  • If the plant is valuable and the freeze will be hard, take cuttings first. A cutting in a jar of water on a warm shelf survives a freeze the mother plant may not.

Cuttings are the cheap insurance policy here — see the philodendron propagation methods guide for where to cut and how to root them.

If you cannot move an outdoor philodendron

Sometimes the plant is in the ground and it is staying there. In zones 10–11, that is usually fine with a little help: pile mulch around the base to insulate the roots, move a potted plant against the house wall under an overhang, and drape frost cloth over the foliage before sunset so it traps the day's warmth. Take the cloth off in the morning so the plant gets light and air. In zone 9, do all of that and accept that a hard freeze may still take the leaves — the roots are the part worth saving, and a thick mulch layer is what saves them. Below zone 9, do not plant a philodendron in the ground and expect it to survive winter; grow it in a container and bring it in.

After cold exposure: a recovery checklist

  1. Move the plant to stable room warmth and bright indirect light. Keep it away from heaters, radiators and hot sun; warming it fast does not help.
  2. Check the pot. If the mix is wet, let it drain and empty the saucer. Do not add water just because the leaves droop.
  3. Inspect the roots and stem base only if the stem is soft or the decline continues. Repot for rotting roots, not for leaf damage alone.
  4. Leave chilled leaves in place for several days, then cut off only tissue that is clearly dead or mushy.
  5. Go back to watering by soil check and hold fertilizer until new growth is steady.

The first 24 hours after a cold snap: an hour-by-hour plan

The first day decides how much of the plant you keep. The rule for all of it: warm it slowly, touch it as little as possible, and let the damage declare itself before you cut anything.

What NOT to do in the first 24 hours

Do not repot it. Do not fertilize it. Do not put it in direct sun to warm it. Do not soak the soil. Do not prune everything that looks bad. Every one of these adds a second stress on top of the cold, and cold-stressed roots absorb slowly, so water and fertilizer sit in the pot and rot them.

Recovery signs: how to tell if your philodendron will make it

Give it two to four weeks in stable warmth before you judge. Damaged leaves do not heal, so you are watching for new growth, not for old leaves recovering. These are the signs that matter:

  • New shoots from the base or from nodes on the stem. This is the clearest sign the plant is alive. A shoot pushing out of a node above the damage means the stem survived there.
  • Stems that stay firm and green when you press them gently. Black, mushy stems are dead tissue.
  • Leaves that stay pliable and keep green tissue, even with browned edges. A leaf that is still partly green is still feeding the plant.
  • Roots that feel moist and firm if you check the pot. Dry, crumbly roots point to loss.

And the point where it is time to let go: stems black and mushy down to the base, a foul smell coming from the pot, and no new growth after four to six weeks in good conditions. At that point, take any healthy cuttings first, then compost the rest.

Examples by situation

  • A Philodendron Brasil near a drafty door may yellow on the side facing cold air while the protected side looks normal.
  • A pot of dense, wet mix is at more risk in a cold spot than a drier pot at the same temperature, because roots slow down and take up less oxygen when cool.
  • One cool night is a different situation from several in a row. If nights keep dropping, move the plant in rather than waiting to see what happens.
  • A plant shipped in winter should be acclimated to room temperature before heavy watering or repotting.

Outdoors and USDA zones: potted vs in-ground

Philodendrons can live outside, but only where winter never gets serious. The table uses USDA average annual minimum temperatures. Your actual microclimate can buy you a few degrees — a sheltered wall, a canopy overhead — and an exposed, windy spot can cost you the same.

USDA zoneAverage annual minimumOutdoor philodendron?
Zone 1140–50°FYes — year-round, the safest zone.
Zone 1030–40°FYes, with care — shelter from wind and cold snaps, and watch winter nights.
Zone 920–30°FMarginal — containers only; protect or bring indoors for winter.
Zone 8 and belowBelow 20°FNo — grow in containers and bring indoors for winter.

Potted and in-ground plants are not the same risk. A pot freezes first because it has no ground heat to draw on, so containers need to come in sooner than anything planted in the ground. In-ground plants in zones 10–11 do best with a canopy overhead and a south-facing wall behind them.

Sources disagree slightly on zone 9: NC State lists heartleaf philodendron for zones 11–12, and Xanadu down to 9b, while UF/IFAS places heartleaf outdoors around zones 10B–11. We use the narrower range, because a dead plant is not a rounding error. If you are in zone 9 and want to try a philodendron outside, grow it in a container you can carry in — and read the winter houseplant care guide for the full cold-season routine.

Moving indoors: when to bring it in, and when it can go back out

Bring it in before nights drop below 60°F, and always before the first frost. Do not wait for the forecast to say freezing — by then the damage is often done on a cold night that never quite reached 32°F.

Before it comes in, check for pests: look under the leaves and at the soil surface, and give the plant a gentle shower or wipe-down. Place it in bright indirect light — the brightest spot you have that is not a cold windowsill. Expect some leaf drop in the first two weeks indoors. That is adjustment to lower light, not failure, and it usually stops on its own.

Moving it back out is the reverse, and it is just as easy to get wrong. Wait until after the last frost and nights stay above 60°F, then harden it off over a week: a few hours in outdoor shade, then longer days, then its summer spot. Never move a plant straight from a dark room into full sun — the leaves will scorch.

Winterizing checklist — run through this in early fall
  • Check night forecasts weekly from September onward.
  • Bring plants in before nights hit 60°F.
  • Inspect every plant for pests before it comes indoors.
  • Move plants away from cold windows and exterior doors.
  • Raise pots off cold tile and concrete floors.
  • Cut watering back as growth slows.
  • Stop fertilizing until spring growth returns.
  • Keep a thermometer at plant level, not across the room.

FAQ

Can philodendrons survive 55°F?

A healthy philodendron may survive a brief dip to about 55°F, but it is a stress zone, not a target. Keep the soil drier and warm the plant promptly.

What temperature kills philodendron?

There is no single universal number, but temperatures near 50°F, frost, or prolonged cold wet conditions can severely damage or kill philodendrons.

Can I leave philodendron outside in winter?

Only in climates where nights stay warm and frost-free. Bring indoor philodendrons inside before nights drop below about 60°F.

What does cold damage look like on philodendron?

Cold damage can show as dark translucent patches, limp leaves, yellowing, browning edges, sudden droop, or later root rot if the soil stayed cold and wet.

Should I repot a cold-damaged philodendron?

Do not repot immediately unless roots are rotting. Stabilize warmth and light first, then inspect roots if decline continues.

Will a philodendron survive a freeze?

No, not reliably. A light frost may only scorch the leaves, but a hard freeze usually kills the plant above ground. A mature tree philodendron may resprout from the base if the roots survived. Bring plants indoors before frost.

Is 60°F too cold for a philodendron at night?

60°F is the caution line, not a disaster. A healthy plant handles a brief night at 60°F if the soil is dry, but repeated nights below 60°F slow growth and raise the risk of chilling injury. Aim for 65°F or warmer at night.

Can a philodendron recover from cold damage?

Often, yes, if the stems and roots are still firm. Move it to stable warmth and bright indirect light, wait several days before pruning, hold fertilizer, and watch for new shoots from the base over the next 2–4 weeks.

What USDA zone can philodendrons grow outside in?

Year-round outdoors is realistic in USDA zones 10–11. Zone 9 is marginal: grow in containers and protect or bring plants in for winter. In colder zones, keep philodendrons in pots and move them indoors for winter.

When should I bring my philodendron indoors for winter?

Before nights drop below 60°F, and always before the first frost. Check for pests first, then place it in bright indirect light away from cold windows.

The short version

  • Keep philodendrons above 60°F, and aim for 65–80°F for everyday growing.
  • A brief dip to 55°F is survivable for a healthy plant; near 50°F or frost is high-risk.
  • Tree philodendrons are the hardiest; variegated types like Pink Princess and Birkin need the warmest spot.
  • Cold wet soil is more dangerous than cool dry soil, and one cold night is different from three in a row.
  • After a cold snap, warm the plant slowly, touch it as little as possible, and wait 2–4 weeks for new shoots before you decide it is gone.
  • Year-round outdoors is realistic only in USDA zones 10–11; in zone 9, grow in containers and bring plants in for winter.
