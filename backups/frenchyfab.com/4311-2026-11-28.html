Quick answer: most healthy French Bulldogs eating a complete and balanced dog food do not need a pile of supplements. The supplement with the best evidence is omega-3 fish oil, which has been shown to help dogs with osteoarthritis and is part of managing allergic skin disease. Glucosamine and chondroitin performed poorly for joint pain in a 2022 review, and the evidence for probiotics in dogs is limited. Always ask your vet before starting a supplement, especially for a dog on medication, and never give human products, which may contain xylitol, a sweetener that is toxic to dogs.

This guide summarises published veterinary research and sources listed at the end, checked on 25 September 2026. It deliberately does not give doses: the right product and amount depend on your dog's weight, health and medications, so agree them with your vet.

Does a French Bulldog need supplements?

A complete and balanced commercial diet is formulated to provide the vitamins and minerals a healthy dog needs. Adding a general multivitamin on top rarely helps and can do harm, because some nutrients are harmful in excess. Supplements make the most sense when they target a specific, diagnosed need, such as arthritis or allergic skin disease, and are chosen with your vet.

French Bulldogs do have breed-specific weak spots. A Royal Veterinary College study of 2,228 French Bulldogs found the most common problems were ear infections (14.0%), diarrhoea (7.5%) and conjunctivitis, with skin fold dermatitis also common. Most of these need a diagnosis and treatment from a vet rather than a supplement.

Joints: omega-3 over glucosamine

French Bulldogs can develop joint and spine problems, including hip dysplasia and intervertebral disc disease. For arthritis-type pain, the strongest evidence among supplements is for omega-3 fatty acids from fish oil. In a randomised, double-blinded trial of 38 dogs with osteoarthritis, dogs fed a diet containing fish-oil omega-3 showed a significant improvement in weight bearing after 90 days. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of 72 trials found clear pain-relief evidence for omega-3-enriched diets and supplements, weak evidence for collagen, and a marked lack of effect for glucosamine-chondroitin products.

Omega-3 does not replace a vet's pain assessment. Limping, reluctance to jump or a hunched back can signal disc disease, which needs prompt veterinary care. Keeping your Frenchie lean does more for joints than any supplement; see how to help an overweight Frenchie and our hip dysplasia guide.

Skin and allergies: fatty acids as one part of the plan

Itchy skin, paw licking and recurrent ear infections are common in French Bulldogs. The International Committee on Allergic Diseases of Animals (ICADA) guidelines for atopic dermatitis describe treatment as multifaceted: identifying and avoiding flare factors, bathing, controlling itch with effective medications, and possibly increasing essential fatty acid intake. In other words, fatty acids can support the skin, but they are not a cure on their own. If your dog is itchy, ask your vet about food allergy trials and itch control; our food allergies guide explains elimination diets.

Gut health: be realistic about probiotics

Probiotics are widely used: in a UK study of acute diarrhoea in dogs, probiotics were the most common management, given in about 60% of cases. The evidence that they change the outcome is weak, though. A systematic review found only a very limited and possibly clinically unimportant effect for acute gut problems, and for chronic gut disease, diet was the key treatment while probiotics added no clear improvement. A probiotic is unlikely to harm a healthy dog, but ongoing diarrhoea, vomiting, blood in the stool or weight loss needs a vet, not a supplement. See our French Bulldog digestive problems guide.

Calming supplements and CBD

Anxiety, noise fear and separation distress respond best to a behaviour plan: gradual training, environmental changes and, where needed, medication prescribed by a vet. Calming chews can be part of that plan but should not be the whole of it. CBD products are unregulated in many places, vary widely in content, and can interact with medications, so only use them if your vet recommends a specific product. Our separation anxiety guide sets out a training plan.

Safety checks before you buy

  • Never use human supplements or sweets as a shortcut. VCA Animal Hospitals lists chewable or gummy vitamins and supplements among the products that can contain xylitol, which is toxic to dogs.
  • Tell your vet about every supplement, especially before surgery or if your dog takes other medication.
  • Choose products made for dogs with a clear ingredient list, the amount of each active ingredient, a lot number and an expiry date. The National Animal Supplement Council runs a quality seal programme in the US.
  • Be wary of big promises. A supplement that claims to cure disease, "detox" or replace veterinary treatment is a red flag.
  • Change one thing at a time and keep notes, so you can tell whether a supplement is making any difference.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best supplement for a French Bulldog?

If your dog needs one at all, omega-3 fish oil has the best overall evidence, for joints and as part of skin care. Ask your vet which product and amount suit your dog.

Should I give my French Bulldog glucosamine?

A 2022 review of the evidence found glucosamine and chondroitin did not relieve osteoarthritis pain and advised against recommending them for that purpose. Talk to your vet about options with better evidence.

Can I give my Frenchie human vitamins?

No. Human products can contain doses or ingredients that are unsafe for dogs, including xylitol in some chewable and gummy vitamins.

Do French Bulldogs need probiotics?

Not routinely. The evidence for probiotics in dogs is limited. Persistent digestive problems need a vet check to find the cause.
