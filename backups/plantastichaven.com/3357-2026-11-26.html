First: What Does a Real Spider Plant Look Like?

Before comparing lookalikes, lock in the original: a real spider plant (Chlorophytum comosum) has narrow arching green leaves with white or cream stripes rising from a central crown, and it dangles baby plantlets on long wiry runners — those “spiders on a thread” give it the name. If your plant matches that silhouette, it is a true spider plant; every plant below imitates some part of it. For the full varietal breakdown see the spider plant family guide.

People searching what does a spider plant look like usually need exactly this silhouette: grassy striped leaves plus hanging babies. If instead you have a cane, a trunk, or purple underside leaves, skip ahead to the lookalike that matches.

Top Spider Plant Look-Alikes at a Glance

These are the houseplants most often mistaken for spider plants, based on leaf shape, growth habit, and overall form — in other words, every plant that looks like a spider plant but belongs to a different genus:

  • Ribbon Plant (Dracaena sanderiana): long, slender green leaves with white or cream stripes; grows upright before arching.
  • Creeping Lily Turf (Liriope muscari): dense, grass-like green foliage in fountain-like clumps; stiffer leaves than a spider plant.
  • Mondo Grass (Ophiopogon japonicus): thick, arching tufts of narrow dark green leaves; mimics a solid-green spider plant's cascading shape.
  • Cast-Iron Plant (Aspidistra elatior): narrow-leaf and variegated cultivars share the clumping, upright-to-arching form and easy-care nature.
  • Airplane Grass (Chlorophytum bichetii / laxum): same genus as the spider plant but more compact and upright, without sprawling runners.
  • Purple Queen (Tradescantia pallida): trailing purple foliage sometimes sold under confusing "spider plant" marketing names.
  • St. Bernard's Lily (Anthericum liliago): grassy, arching leaves with small white flowers; a classic old-world look-alike.
  • Variegated Flax Lily (Dianella tasmanica): strappy, striped leaves that echo a variegated spider plant's foliage.

Each of these shares the long, arching, ribbon-like leaf shape that defines the spider plant's silhouette. Detailed side-by-side comparisons — including pet safety, light needs, and growth differences — are in the full comparison tables below.

How to identify plants that look like spider plants

For indoor plants similar to spider plants, first separate stemless rosettes from plants with a cane or trunk. A dragon tree has leaves clustered on a woody cane; purple heart has jointed purple stems; and yucca develops stiff, sword-like leaves. These features rule out a true spider plant.

An outdoor plant that looks like a spider plant is often liriope, mondo grass (Ophiopogon), flax lily, or a Carex sedge. These grassy perennials form landscape clumps but do not make spiderettes. This distinction is more reliable than color alone because green and variegated cultivars occur across several unrelated genera.

Why Spider Plant Lookalikes Exist

The spider plant — Chlorophytum comosum — is one of the most widely grown houseplants in the world. Its graceful, arching foliage, ease of care, prolific propagation, and ASPCA-listed non-toxic status for cats and dogs have made it a fixture in homes, offices, and public spaces for well over a century. It is so familiar and so widely distributed that plants with superficially similar appearances have been routinely misidentified as spider plants by garden centers, plant shops, and even experienced growers who encounter an unfamiliar lookalike for the first time.

The lookalike problem is also structural: the horticultural industry has long used broad descriptive labels — "tropical foliage," "grass-like plant," "trailing houseplant" — that do not distinguish between botanically unrelated species. When you combine familiar plant labels that are not scientifically precise with the fact that many unrelated plants evolved similar leaf shapes in response to similar environmental pressures (a process called convergent evolution), you get a persistent identification challenge that has confused plant lovers for generations.

This guide helps you narrow it down. For each lookalike, you will find a clear identification framework, the key distinguishing feature that separates it from a true spider plant, its care requirements in brief, and a note on pet safety — because the spider plant's pet-safe status is one of its most important practical advantages, and several of its lookalikes do not share that advantage. Every plant that looks like a spider plant but isn't gives itself away somewhere — you just need to know where to check.

The Five-Clue Identification Framework

Before going through each individual lookalike, here is the master identification framework you can use on any plant that looks like it might be a spider plant. Work through these five clues in order — the first one that definitively matches or rejects is usually enough to identify the plant. If you are comparing a spider plant look alike against the real thing — or hunting spider plant similar plants to add to your collection — the leaf, root, and plantlet clues below settle it fast.

Clue 1: Look for the Plantlets

The single most definitive feature of a true spider plant is the production of baby plantlets on the ends of long arching flower stems. Once your spider plant matures (usually one to two years after planting), it sends out slender stems that arch outward and downward. Small white star-shaped flowers appear on these stems, and after flowering, small baby spider plants — called plantlets or pups — develop at the tips. These plantlets dangle in the air and can be rooted to start new plants.

None of the lookalikes compared below produces spiderettes in the same pattern. If a mature plant has dangling baby rosettes on arching stems, it is very likely a spider plant. A young, recently divided, or stressed spider plant may not have spiderettes yet, so their absence does not rule it out; continue to Clue 2.

Clue 2: Check the Leaf Arrangement and Center of the Plant

Spider plants grow in a dense, symmetrical rosette from the center of the plant. There is no visible central stem or trunk. The leaves emerge from a single central point and arch outward in all directions, creating a fountain-like shape. If you look at the plant from above, you see a circular, grass-like clump.

Most spider plant lookalikes do not have this rosette growth habit. Dracaenas have a visible central cane with leaves at the top. Yuccas have a trunk. Liriope grows in neat grass-like clumps but does not have the arching, fountain-like spread of a spider plant. Flax lily has a more upright, iris-like growth habit. If your plant has a visible trunk, cane, or rhizome system that is not a rosette, it is not a spider plant.

Clue 3: Examine the Roots

Spider plants develop thick, fleshy, cream-colored storage roots around the crown. These roots support the identification, but they are not proof by themselves: liriope and mondo grass can also have swollen or rhizomatous underground structures. Confirm a spider plant by combining the fleshy roots with a stemless rosette and, on a mature plant, arching stems that carry flowers or spiderettes. Avoid unpotting a healthy plant solely to identify it.

Clue 4: Treat Pet Safety as a Check, Not a Test

The ASPCA lists the spider plant as non-toxic to cats and dogs, and that matters once you know what you have. It is not an identification test, though. Safe and toxic plants can look alike, and a plant tag can be wrong. Identify the plant with the other clues first, then look up its exact botanical name in the ASPCA database. If a pet may have chewed a plant you cannot identify, call a veterinarian or poison-control service and describe the plant rather than assuming it is safe.

Clue 5: Look at the Leaf Shape and Color in Detail

Spider plant leaves are long and slender (up to 18 inches), tapered at both ends, and have a distinctly grass-like texture. The standard variegated spider plant has white or cream edges with a green center. The leaves arch gracefully and are relatively uniform in width throughout their length. They are not thick, fleshy, or succulent in texture. If a plant has thick, waxy, or succulent-like leaves; a central rib that is prominent; or a leaf shape that is more lance-shaped or sword-shaped than grass-like, it is not a spider plant.

1. Dracaena Marginata (Dragon Tree)

Dracaena marginata, commonly called the dragon tree or Madagascar dragon tree, is the spider plant lookalike most frequently encountered in homes and garden centers. Its long, narrow, dark-green leaves with red or burgundy margins arch outward from the top of slender cane-like stems in a way that can resemble a spider plant, particularly when the dracaena is young and has not yet developed the pronounced cane structure that makes mature specimens so distinctive.

Tradescantia pallida — commonly called Purple Heart, Purple Queen, or inch plant — is the plant most frequently mislabeled as a "purple spider plant." It has trailing stems with pointed, lance-shaped leaves that are a striking deep purple to magenta color. 3. Liriope (Lilyturf) and Mondo Grass

Liriope (lilyturf) and mondo grass look similar but belong to different genera: Liriope and Ophiopogon. Both are grass-like landscape perennials that form dense clumps of strap-shaped leaves. Their hardiness varies by species and cultivar. Compared with spider plants, their leaves are usually more upright, they produce flower spikes rather than spiderettes, and they are primarily grown outdoors.

4. Flax Lily (Dianella)

Dianella — commonly called flax lily, blueberry lily, or native flax — is an ornamental grass-like plant native to Australia, Southeast Asia, and Africa. It produces strappy, iris-like leaves in shades of green, blue-green, or variegated with white or cream. Like liriope, it is primarily an outdoor plant but is sometimes used as an indoor specimen. Its leaves are more rigid and iris-like than spider plant leaves, and it produces distinctive blue or purple berries that spider plants never produce.

5. Airplane Plant / ‘Vittatum’ Spider Plant

“Airplane plant” and “ribbon plant” are common names for the true spider plant, not separate species. They are often applied to variegated forms such as Chlorophytum comosum ‘Vittatum,’ whose leaves have a broad pale center stripe and green edges. These names belong in this guide because retail labels can make the plant seem different. It has the same stemless rosette, fleshy storage roots, care needs, and ability to produce spiderettes as other spider plants.

Variegation depends on the cultivar. ‘Vittatum’ has a broad white or cream center stripe with green edges, while ‘Variegatum’ typically has pale margins and a green center. Both are true spider plants.

6. Yucca (Yucca Elephantipes and other species)

Yucca plants — particularly Yucca elephantipes, the spineless yucca or elephant yucca — have long, sword-shaped leaves that can superficially resemble spider plant foliage, especially when the yucca is young and has not yet developed its full trunk structure. However, yucca leaves are much thicker, more rigid, and more sharply pointed than spider plant leaves, and many yucca species have a sharp terminal spine at the tip. Yucca elephantipes, the spineless yucca, lacks that spike but its leaves are still stiff. Yuccas also grow into distinctive tree-like forms over time, with thick woody trunks and leaf clusters at the top.

7. Sedge Grasses (Carex species)

Sedge grasses in the Carex genus produce clumps of arching, strap-shaped leaves that can look somewhat similar to spider plant foliage at a glance. However, sedge grasses have triangular stems (a botanical feature that distinguishes them from grasses, which have round or flat stems), and they do not produce plantlets or grow in the rosette formation characteristic of spider plants. Many species spread by rhizomes. Most sedge grasses are outdoor plants used in landscape plantings and are less commonly grown as indoor houseplants.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the plant that looks like a spider plant but has purple leaves?

The plant most often described as a “purple spider plant” is Tradescantia pallida, commonly called Purple Heart or Purple Queen. It is not a spider plant: it trails on jointed stems, has vivid purple foliage, and belongs to the Commelinaceae family rather than Asparagaceae. Its sap may irritate skin and pets, so place it out of reach and contact a veterinarian or poison-control service after a suspected exposure. See our purple spider plant identification guide.

What is the difference between spider plant and airplane plant?

They are the same plant. Airplane plant and ribbon plant are common names for Chlorophytum comosum, the true spider plant, not separate lookalikes. The cultivar ‘Vittatum’ usually has a broad pale center stripe and green leaf edges.

What does a dracaena look like compared to a spider plant?

Dracaenas — especially Dracaena marginata — look similar to spider plants from a distance because both have long, arching, strap-shaped leaves. However, at closer inspection, the differences are clear. A dracaena has a visible central woody cane or trunk with leaves clustered at the top, and the stem is bare at the bottom on mature plants. A spider plant has no trunk — all leaves emerge from a dense central rosette with no visible stem. Dracaenas also do not produce plantlets and do not have the thick rhizomes that spider plants have. See our complete spider plant vs dracaena comparison for the full breakdown.

Is liriope the same as spider plant?

No. Liriope (lilyturf) and mondo grass (Ophiopogon) are separate genera of grass-like perennial plants that are botanically unrelated to spider plants (Chlorophytum comosum). While both have strap-shaped leaves and both are green, liriope leaves are more upright and rigid, grow in compact clumps, and do not arch or cascade the way spider plant leaves do. Liriope produces flower spikes in purple, blue, or white above the foliage and is primarily used as a landscape groundcover rather than a houseplant.

How can I tell if my plant is a real spider plant?

No single clue is definitive, so combine them. On a mature plant, look for dangling plantlets (spiderettes) on long arching stems; a young or stressed plant may not have any yet. Check for a stemless rosette with no visible cane or trunk. Fleshy, cream-colored storage roots support the identification, but liriope and mondo grass can also have swollen roots, so do not unpot a healthy plant just to check. A plant that fits all of these is very likely a true spider plant; if you are still unsure, confirm the botanical name with a local nursery or extension service.

Are spider plant lookalikes safe for pets?

Do not assume a lookalike shares the spider plant’s safety profile. The ASPCA lists Chlorophytum comosum as non-toxic to cats and dogs, but lists Dracaena marginata and yucca species as toxic. Safety information for other lookalikes varies by exact species and exposure. Confirm the botanical name, check the ASPCA database, and contact a veterinarian or poison-control service if a pet may have eaten a plant.

Can spider plant lookalikes purify air like spider plants?

Spider plants and some dracaenas removed certain volatile compounds in sealed-chamber experiments, but those studies do not show that a practical number of houseplants can clean the air in a normal occupied room. Keep plants for their horticultural and wellbeing value; for indoor air quality, the U.S. EPA prioritizes source control, appropriate ventilation, and effective filtration.

Why is my spider plant not producing plantlets?

A young spider plant may not be mature enough to flower and form spiderettes. Low light, irregular watering, recent division, or excess nitrogen can also favor leaf growth over flowering. Provide bright indirect light, water when the upper potting mix begins to dry, and avoid overfertilizing. Slight root snugness is normal, but repot if the container is distorted, roots displace most of the mix, or water runs through without wetting it evenly. Production timing varies by plant and growing conditions.
